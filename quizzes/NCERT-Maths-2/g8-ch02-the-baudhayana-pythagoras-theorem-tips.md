# Tips & Tricks — The Baudhāyana-Pythagoras Theorem (NCERT Grade 8, Ganita Prakash Part 2, Ch. 2)

Nine short tricks pulled straight from the chapter. Each one tells you *why* it works, shows a worked example, and leaves you one to try.

---

## 1. Double a square with its diagonal, not its side

**The trick:** To make a square with *double* the area, use the **diagonal** of the old square as the new side. Never double the side.

**Why it works:** Doubling the side doubles the length *and* the width, so the area becomes 2 × 2 = **4** times bigger. But cut the square along both diagonals and you get 2 congruent triangles; the square on the diagonal is made of 4 of those same triangles — exactly twice as many.

**Worked example:** A square has side 6, area 36. The square on its diagonal has area 2 × 36 = 72. (The square of side 12 would have area 144 — four times, not two.)

**Try it yourself:** A square has area 50 sq cm. What is the area of the square built on its diagonal? And of the square whose side is twice as long?

---

## 2. Halve a square by joining the midpoints

**The trick:** Join the midpoints of the four sides. The tilted square you get has exactly **half** the area.

**Why it works:** It is Trick 1 run backwards — the big square's side is the tilted square's diagonal. Folding the four corners in to the centre makes the four corner triangles cover the tilted square exactly.

**Worked example:** Square of side 10 → area 100. Midpoint square → area 50. (A square of side 5 would be only 25 — a quarter, because half the side means a quarter of the area.)

**Try it yourself:** A square paper has side 12 cm. What is the area of the square formed by joining the midpoints of its sides? How many squares of side 6 cm would fit in the original?

---

## 3. Isosceles right triangle: hypotenuse = side × √2

**The trick:** If the two equal sides are *a*, the hypotenuse is **a√2**, because c² = 2a².

**Why it works:** The square on the hypotenuse is the "doubled square" of Trick 1, so its area is 2a². Taking the square root gives c = a√2. Going backwards: a² = c² ÷ 2.

**Worked example:** Equal sides 12 → c² = 2 × 144 = 288 → c = √288, between 16 and 17 (16² = 256, 17² = 289). Hypotenuse √72 → a² = 36 → a = 6.

**Try it yourself:** The hypotenuse of an isosceles right triangle is 20. Find the square of each equal side, then say which two whole numbers the side lies between.

---

## 4. Trap √2 between two decimals

**The trick:** To pin down √2 (or any square root), square the two neighbouring decimals and check which squares sit on either side of 2.

**Why it works:** If 1.4² = 1.96 is below 2 and 1.5² = 2.25 is above 2, the number whose square is exactly 2 must be between 1.4 and 1.5. Add one more decimal place and repeat: 1.41² = 1.9881, 1.42² = 2.0164, so 1.41 < √2 < 1.42. Then 1.414 < √2 < 1.415.

**Worked example:** Bounds for √18 (the hypotenuse when the equal sides are 3): 4.2² = 17.64 < 18 < 18.49 = 4.3², so 4.2 < √18 < 4.3.

**Try it yourself:** Find one-decimal bounds for √32 (the hypotenuse when the equal sides are 4). Check both squares.

## 5. Hypotenuse known? SUBTRACT the squares

**The trick:** a² + b² = c² — the square on the **hypotenuse** is the *sum*. If you already know the hypotenuse and want a leg, **subtract**: b² = c² − a².

**Why it works:** The hypotenuse is the longest side (c² is bigger than a² and b²), so its square must be the total of the other two squares. Adding when you should subtract gives a number far too big.

**Worked example:** Leg 9, hypotenuse 15: b² = 225 − 81 = 144 → b = 12. (Adding gives √306 — wrong.) Leg 8, hypotenuse 17: 289 − 64 = 225 → 15.

**Try it yourself:** A right triangle has one leg 40 and hypotenuse 41. Find the other leg.

## 6. Spot a triple, then scale it

**The trick:** Memorise the primitive triples (3, 4, 5), (5, 12, 13), (8, 15, 17), (7, 24, 25). Any multiple of a triple — (6, 8, 10), (9, 12, 15), (30, 40, 50) — is also a triple.

**Why it works:** (ka)² + (kb)² = k²(a² + b²) = k²c² = (kc)². The common factor k² pulls straight out, so the equation still balances.

**Worked example:** Is (15, 36, 39) a right triangle? Divide by 3 → (5, 12, 13). Yes! Is (10, 24, 25)? 100 + 576 = 676 = 26², not 25² — no.

**Try it yourself:** Which of these are Baudhāyana triples: (12, 16, 20), (21, 28, 35), (10, 15, 20)? Find the primitive triple each one scales from, if any.

## 7. Make new triples from odd squares

**The trick:** Pick an odd number that is a perfect square (9, 25, 49, 81, 121, …). Write it as 2n − 1, find n, and the triple is (√odd, n − 1, n).

**Why it works:** The sum of the first n odd numbers is n², so (n − 1)² + (2n − 1) = n². When the nth odd number 2n − 1 is itself a square, you have two squares adding to a square.

**Worked example:** 49 = 2n − 1 → n = 25 → triple (7, 24, 25): 49 + 576 = 625 ✓. 81 → n = 41 → (9, 40, 41).

**Try it yourself:** Use 121 to build a triple, then check it by squaring.

---

## 8. Rhombus and rectangle: look for the hidden right triangle

**The trick:** The diagonals of a rhombus cut each other in **half** at right angles — so use *half* of each diagonal as the legs. For a rectangle, the diagonal is the hypotenuse of a triangle made by two adjacent sides.

**Why it works:** The two half-diagonals and one side of the rhombus form a right triangle; the side is the hypotenuse. In a rectangle every corner is a right angle, so length, width and diagonal form a right triangle.

**Worked example:** Rhombus diagonals 24 and 70 → legs 12 and 35 → side √(144 + 1225) = √1369 = 37. (Using the whole diagonals gives 74 — a common slip.) Rectangle 7 by 24 → diagonal 25.

**Try it yourself:** A rhombus has diagonals 16 and 30. Find its side. Then find the diagonal of a 9 by 12 rectangle.

---

## 9. Turn a word problem into a triangle (the lotus trick)

**The trick:** When something upright *tilts* until its tip touches the ground or water, the tilted length is the **hypotenuse** and equals the full height. Set the unknown as *x* and let the algebra cancel.

**Why it works:** The upright stem (length x + 1) is perpendicular to the water; when it sways, its tip moves 3 units sideways and the whole stem becomes the hypotenuse. So 3² + x² = (x + 1)². The x² terms cancel, leaving a simple equation: 9 = 2x + 1, so x = 4.

**Worked example:** Depth of the Līlāvatī lake = 4 units; total stem = 5 units — the 3-4-5 triangle in disguise!

**Try it yourself:** A reed stands 2 units above the water. When it sways, its tip touches the water 6 units from where it stood. Set up the equation and find the depth.

### Quick-check card

- Double area → build on the **diagonal**. Half area → join **midpoints**.
- Isosceles right triangle: **c = a√2**, **a² = c² ÷ 2**.
- Hypotenuse is the **longest** side: unknown hypotenuse → **add** squares; unknown leg → **subtract**.
- Know (3, 4, 5), (5, 12, 13), (8, 15, 17), (7, 24, 25) — and their multiples.
- √2 ≈ 1.414…, never ends, never a fraction.
