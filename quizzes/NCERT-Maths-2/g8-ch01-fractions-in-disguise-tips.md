# Fractions in Disguise — Tips & Tricks (NCERT Grade 8, Ganita Prakash Part 2, Chapter 1)

Percentages are just fractions wearing a mask: the bottom number is always 100. Once you see
that, every trick below is really a fraction trick.

## 1. "Per cent" = "out of 100" — write the fraction first

**Why it works:** *Per centum* is Latin for "by the hundred", so x% means x/100. Any percentage
question becomes a fraction question the moment you write the hidden 100.

**Worked example:** What is 24% as a fraction? Write 24/100, then simplify: 24/100 = 12/50 = 6/25.
And the other way: 3/4 → multiply top and bottom by 25 → 75/100 → 75%.

**Try it yourself:** Write 45% as a fraction in simplest form, and write 7/20 as a percentage.

## 2. Build any percentage from 10%, 5% and 1%

**Why it works:** 10% is one tenth (move the decimal point one place left), 5% is half of 10%,
1% is a hundredth. Since (20% of y) + (5% of y) = 25% of y, you can add and double these pieces
to reach any percentage.

**Worked example:** 15% of 80 → 10% of 80 = 8, 5% of 80 = 4, so 15% of 80 = 12.
40% of 287 → 10% = 28.7, 20% = 57.4, 40% = 114.8.

**Try it yourself:** Find 35% of 240 mentally (hint: 25% + 10%).

## 3. Use the "friendly fraction" shortcuts: 25% = ¼, 50% = ½, 75% = ¾

**Why it works:** 25/100 = 1/4, so 25% of a number is the same as dividing by 4 — no multiplying
by 25 and dividing by 100 needed.

**Worked example:** 25% of 287 = 287 ÷ 4 = 71.75. 75% of 36 = 3 × (36 ÷ 4) = 27.
80% of 75 = (4/5) × 75 = 60 — exactly the A-grade cut-off in the chapter.

**Try it yourself:** Find 75% of 128 and 12.5% of 96 using fraction shortcuts.

## 4. Big percentage? Subtract from 100 instead

**Why it works:** 90% = 100% − 10%, so 90% of a number is the number minus one tenth of it.
This is also how the chapter finds the "remaining" part: if 40% of the journey is done, 60% is
left.

**Worked example:** 90% of 460 = 460 − 46 = 414. If 40% of the distance is 92 km, then 20% is
46 km and the remaining 60% is 92 + 46 = 138 km.

**Try it yourself:** Find 95% of 640, and 85% of 200, by subtracting.

## 5. Swap the numbers: x% of y = y% of x

**Why it works:** x% of y = (x × y)/100, and y% of x = (y × x)/100 — the same product, so the
same answer. Pick whichever order is easier.

**Worked example:** 12% of 25 looks awkward, but 25% of 12 is just 12 ÷ 4 = 3. Likewise
60% of 15 = 15% of 60 = 9.

**Try it yourself:** Find 8% of 50 and 36% of 25 by swapping.

## 6. Ratio to percentage: compare the part to the WHOLE

**Why it works:** A ratio like millet : water = 2 : 7 compares two parts to each other. The
mixture has 2 + 7 = 9 parts, so millet is 2/9 of it, not 2/7.

**Worked example:** Millet share = 2/9 × 100 = 22.22%, water = 77.78%. In 500 ml of kanji, millet
= 22.22% of 500 ≈ 111 ml. (Estimate first: 2/9 is a bit under ¼, so a bit under 125 ml.)

**Try it yourself:** Sand and cement are mixed 3 : 5. What percentage of the mix is cement?

## 7. Percentage change: always divide by the ORIGINAL

**Why it works:** "Increased by 40%" means the increase is 40% of where you *started*. The
base is the old value, never the new one — otherwise the same price rise gives two different
answers.

**Worked example:** Tomatoes ₹30 → ₹42: increase 12, 12/30 × 100 = 40%. Footfall 160 → 100:
decrease 60, 60/160 × 100 = 37.5%. Note that going back from 100 to 160 is a 60% increase, not
37.5% — the base changed!

**Try it yourself:** Petrol went from ₹60 to ₹100. What is the percentage increase? (Watch the
base.)

## 8. Multiply by (1 + r) for growth and (1 − r) for decline

**Why it works:** "Increase by 20%" means you keep 100% and add 20%, i.e. multiply by 1.20.
"Decrease by 18%" means 82% remains, i.e. multiply by 0.82. One multiplication does the whole
job and never uses the wrong base.

**Worked example:** A vase bought at ₹2650 sold at an 18% loss: 0.82 × 2650 = ₹2173.
A number increased by 20% becomes 90: 1.2 × n = 90, so n = 90 ÷ 1.2 = 75 (not 90 − 18 = 72!).

**Try it yourself:** A car was bought for ₹4,40,000 after a 15% discount. What was the original
price? (Which number do you divide by?)

## 9. Successive percentages MULTIPLY — they do not add

**Why it works:** The second change is applied to an already-changed amount. A 30% discount
followed by 20% leaves 0.7 × 0.8 = 0.56 of the price — a 44% discount, not 50%. A 50% markup
followed by a 50% discount leaves 1.5 × 0.5 = 0.75 — a 25% loss, not break-even.

**Worked example:** Cakely's "30% + 20%" on a ₹200 cake: 200 → 140 → 112. Cakify's flat 50%:
200 → 100. Cakify is cheaper. Bus fares up 3% then 4%: 1.03 × 1.04 = 1.0712, a 7.12% rise.

**Try it yourself:** A shopkeeper adds a 35% margin and then gives a 30% discount. Profit or
loss, and by what percentage?

## 10. Compounding: the interest earns interest

**Why it works:** Without compounding, the principal never changes, so every year adds the same
interest: amount = p(1 + rt), a straight-line growth. With compounding, each year's interest
is added back, so the next year's interest is bigger: amount = p(1 + r)ᵗ, which grows faster
and faster.

**Worked example:** ₹6000 at 10% p.a. for 3 years. Without compounding: 6000 + 3 × 600 = ₹7800
(a 30% gain). With compounding: 6000 × 1.1 × 1.1 × 1.1 = 6000 × 1.331 = ₹7986 (a 33.1% gain).
₹1000 at 10% doubles in 10 years without compounding, but in under 8 years with it.

**Try it yourself:** ₹20,000 at 10% p.a. for 2 years — how much more does compounding give
than simple interest?

### Quick reminder card

| Want to… | Do this |
|---|---|
| Turn a fraction into % | multiply by 100 |
| Turn % into a fraction | put it over 100 and simplify |
| Find 10% / 5% / 1% | ÷10, then halve, or ÷100 |
| Increase by r% | multiply by (1 + r/100) |
| Decrease by r% | multiply by (1 − r/100) |
| % change | (change ÷ original) × 100 |
| Two changes in a row | multiply the two multipliers |
| Compound growth for t years | p × (1 + r)ᵗ |
