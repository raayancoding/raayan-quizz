# Algebra Play — Tricks & Tips (NCERT Grade 8, Ganita Prakash Part 2, Chapter 6)

## 1. Catch the trick with a letter-number
**Why it works:** Whenever a "Think of a Number" trick always lands on the same answer, the chosen number must have cancelled out. Replace it with *x* and follow the steps — if no *x* is left at the end, the trick can never fail.

**Worked example:** Double, add 4, halve, subtract the original:
x → 2x → 2x + 4 → x + 2 → x + 2 − x = **2**.

**Try it yourself:** Think of a number, multiply by 4, add 12, divide by 4, subtract the original. Use *x* to predict the answer before you test it.

## 2. Design your own ending
**Why it works:** In "double, add k, halve, subtract", the k you add gets halved. So the final answer is always k ÷ 2 — pick k to get whatever ending you like.

**Worked example:** Want the trick to end in 3? Add 6: (2x + 6) ÷ 2 − x = 3.

**Try it yourself:** What should you add so the trick ends in 7?

## 3. Read a date out of 100M + D
**Why it works:** The date trick quietly builds 100M + 165 + D. Subtract 165 and you get 100M + D. Because a day is at most 31, it only ever needs two digits — so the last two digits are the day and whatever is in front is the month.

**Worked example:** Final answer 1390 → 1390 − 165 = 1225 → month **12**, day **25**.

**Try it yourself:** A friend reports 1077. Which date did they pick?

## 4. The middle counts twice in a pyramid
**Why it works:** In a three-row pyramid with bottom row a, b, c the middle numbers are a + b and b + c. Adding them, b appears in both — so the top is a + 2b + c. For four rows it's a + 3b + 3c + d.

**Worked example:** Bottom 8, 13, 4 → top = 8 + 2 × 13 + 4 = **38**, no need to build the rows.

**Try it yourself:** Bottom row 9, 6, 15 — what is the top?

## 5. Work backwards with subtraction
**Why it works:** The pyramid rule says *below + below = above*, so a missing lower box is *above − the other lower box*. Fill every box you can by subtracting, then use a letter-number for the rest.

**Worked example:** Top 60, bottom 12, c, 8: (12 + c) + (c + 8) = 60 → 20 + 2c = 60 → c = **20**.

**Try it yourself:** Top 44, bottom row 6, ?, 10. Find the missing number.

## 6. Calendar squares: the sum is 4 × (top-left + 4)
**Why it works:** In a 2 × 2 calendar grid the numbers are a, a + 1, a + 7, a + 8 (the row below is one week later). Their sum is 4a + 16. So divide the sum by 4 and subtract 4 to find the top-left number.

**Worked example:** Sum 36 → 36 ÷ 4 = 9 → 9 − 4 = **5**, so the grid is 5, 6, 12, 13.

**Try it yourself:** Your friend's 2 × 2 grid adds to 88. Which four dates did they circle?

## 7. Start a shape grid with the one-shape row
**Why it works:** A row with only one kind of shape gives its value straight away (★ + ★ + ★ = 27 → ★ = 9). Feed that value into the other rows and the rest unravel one at a time.

**Worked example:** ★★★ = 27 → ★ = 9; then ●●★ = 19 → 2● = 10 → ● = **5**.

**Try it yourself:** ▲▲▲ = 36, ▲■■ = 22, ■◆◆ = 15. Find ◆.

## 8. Biggest product: big digit multiplies, others go downhill
**Why it works:** For digits p < q < r in □□ × □, the products share their tens-part and differ only in the units-part. Making the largest digit the multiplier and arranging the other two in decreasing order wins every comparison: qp × r.

**Worked example:** Digits 2, 3, 5 → 32 × 5 = **160**, beating 52 × 3 = 156.

**Try it yourself:** Digits 2, 6, 9 — which arrangement gives the largest product?

## 9. Reverse the digits: 9 for differences, 11 for sums
**Why it works:** A two-digit number is 10a + b; its reverse is 10b + a. Subtract and you get 9(b − a); add and you get 11(a + b). So the difference ÷ 9 is the digit difference, and the sum ÷ 11 is the digit sum.

**Worked example:** 74 − 47 = 27 = 9 × 3 (7 − 4); 47 + 74 = 121 = 11 × 11 (4 + 7).

**Try it yourself:** For 85 and 58, predict the difference ÷ 9 and the sum ÷ 11 before calculating.

## 10. The 1001 trick: abcabc = abc × 7 × 11 × 13
**Why it works:** Writing abc twice is the same as multiplying by 1001, and 1001 = 7 × 11 × 13. Dividing by those three primes in turn just undoes the multiplication.

**Worked example:** 123123 ÷ 7 ÷ 11 ÷ 13 = **123**.

**Try it yourself:** Without dividing, what is 456456 ÷ 1001? And is 456456 divisible by 91?
