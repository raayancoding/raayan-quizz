# Proportional Reasoning–2 — Tricks & Tips (NCERT Grade 8, Ganita Prakash Part 2, Ch. 3)

## 1. The cross-multiply handshake
**Why it works:** Two ratios a : b and c : d are proportional only when a × d = b × c. Multiplying "crosswise" compares both ratios on the same scale, so if the products match, the ratios are the same fraction in disguise.

**Worked example:** Are 6 : 3 and 4 : 2 proportional? 6 × 2 = 12 and 3 × 4 = 12. Equal — so yes, both are really 2 : 1.

**Try it yourself:** Are 9 : 6 and 12 : 8 proportional?

## 2. Drop five zeros: map scale to kilometres
**Why it works:** An RF like 1 : 60,00,000 is in centimetres. There are 100 cm in a metre and 1,000 m in a kilometre, so cm → km means dividing by 1,00,000 — i.e. knock off five zeros.

**Worked example:** RF 1 : 60,00,000 → 60,00,000 cm = 60 km. So a 4.5 cm gap on the map is 4.5 × 60 = 270 km (as the crow flies — not by road!).

**Try it yourself:** A map has RF 1 : 25,00,000. Two towns are 3 cm apart on it. How far apart are they really?

## 3. Scale every term, never add to it
**Why it works:** A ratio with many terms (like 8 : 4 : 2 : 1) stays the same recipe only if every term is multiplied by the same factor. Adding the same number to each term wrecks the proportion.

**Worked example:** Puneet has 2 chillies instead of 4 — that's a factor of ½. So he uses 8 × ½ = 4 coriander, 2 × ½ = 1 toor dal and 1 × ½ = 0.5 fenugreek: 4 : 2 : 1 : 0.5.

**Try it yourself:** Yasmin's purple paint is red : blue : white :: 2 : 3 : 5. She has 15 L of blue. How much red and white does she need?

## 4. Find "one part" first
**Why it works:** To split a whole in a ratio, add the terms to get the number of parts, divide the whole by that sum to find the size of one part, then multiply. Everything else falls out automatically.

**Worked example:** Split 110 units of concrete in 1 : 1.5 : 3. Terms add to 5.5; 110 ÷ 5.5 = 20 per part. Cement 20, sand 30, gravel 60 — check: 20 + 30 + 60 = 110.

**Try it yourself:** 100 coins are in the ratio ₹10 : ₹5 : ₹2 : ₹1 :: 4 : 3 : 2 : 1. How many of each coin are there, and how much money is it in total?

## 5. Triangles share 180°, pies share 360°
**Why it works:** "Dividing a whole in a ratio" works for angles too. The whole is 180° for a triangle's angles and 360° for a pie chart, so one part = (180° or 360°) ÷ (sum of terms).

**Worked example:** Angles 1 : 3 : 5 → 9 parts, 180° ÷ 9 = 20°, so 20°, 60°, 100°. Grades 6 : 5 : 4 : 3 : 2 → 20 parts, 360° ÷ 20 = 18°, so 108°, 90°, 72°, 54°, 36°.

**Try it yourself:** A triangle's angles are in the ratio 2 : 3 : 4. What are they?

## 6. Simplify a long ratio with the HCF
**Why it works:** Dividing all the terms by their highest common factor keeps the proportion exactly the same but makes the numbers small, so the "one part" step is quicker and less error-prone.

**Worked example:** 12 : 10 : 8 : 6 : 4 — the HCF is 2, giving 6 : 5 : 4 : 3 : 2. Now the parts add to 20 instead of 40, and 360 ÷ 20 = 18 is an easy number.

**Try it yourself:** Simplify 15 : 25 : 35 : 45 and say how many parts it has.

## 7. Ask "same direction or opposite?" before choosing the method
**Why it works:** If both quantities grow together (more pencils, more money) it's direct proportion and the quotient x ÷ y stays fixed. If one grows while the other shrinks (more workers, fewer days) it's inverse proportion and the product x × y stays fixed. Choosing the wrong one gives an answer that is clearly silly — faster car, longer trip!

**Worked example:** 24 pencils cost ₹120 → 20 pencils? Both shrink together: ₹5 each, so ₹100. 20 workers take 4 days → 10 workers? Fewer workers, more days: 20 × 4 = 10 × d, d = 8 days.

**Try it yourself:** A pump fills 2 tanks in 6 hours. How long for 5 tanks — and is this direct or inverse?

## 8. Inverse proportion = keep the product constant
**Why it works:** For inverse proportion, x₁ × y₁ = x₂ × y₂ = k. In speed–time problems k is the distance; in workers–days problems k is the total "worker-days" of effort. Find k first, then divide.

**Worked example:** 42 machines × 63 days = 2,646 machine-days. To finish in 54 days: 2,646 ÷ 54 = 49 machines. A car at 60 km/h for 2 h covers 120 km; at 80 km/h that takes 120 ÷ 80 = 1.5 h.

**Try it yourself:** A school has 8 periods of 45 minutes. If it has 9 periods in the same school day, how long is each one?

## 9. Working together: add the rates, not the times
**Why it works:** Someone who finishes a job in t hours does 1/t of it per hour. Two people's "per hour" fractions can be added; their times cannot. The combined time is 1 ÷ (combined rate), and it is always less than the faster person's time alone.

**Worked example:** Ram takes 1 h (rate 1 per hour), Shyam takes 1.5 h (rate 2/3 per hour). Together: 1 + 2/3 = 5/3 per hour, so the job takes 3/5 h = 36 minutes.

**Try it yourself:** A small pump fills a tank in 3 hours and a large one in 2 hours. How long do both together take?

## 10. Sanity-check with a factor
**Why it works:** Proportions are about factors (×2, ×½, ×12), not differences (+3, −12). Before trusting an answer, ask: "By what factor did the first quantity change? Did the second change by the same factor (direct) or its reciprocal (inverse)?"

**Worked example:** Walking (5 km/h) to car (60 km/h) is ×12 in speed, so the time should be ÷12: 18 h → 1.5 h. An answer like "18 − 12 = 6 h" fails the check.

**Try it yourself:** 2 pumps fill a tank in 18 hours. If 4 more identical pumps are added, by what factor does the time change, and what is the new filling time?
