# Tales by Dots and Lines — Tips & Tricks (NCERT Grade 8, Ganita Prakash Part 2, Ch 5)

## 1. The mean is a balance point — check it with distances
**Why it works:** The mean is the one value where the total distance to the numbers on its left equals the total distance to the numbers on its right. So you can *verify* a mean without re-adding everything.

**Worked example:** For 10, 10, 11, 17 the mean is 12. Left distances: 2 + 2 + 1 = 5. Right distance: 17 − 12 = 5. Equal — so 12 really is the centre.

**Try it yourself:** Is 9 the mean of 5, 8, 10, 13? Check with distances instead of adding.

## 2. New value above the mean? Mean goes up. Below? Mean goes down. Equal? Nothing happens
**Why it works:** A new value adds distance to one side of the balance, so the mean must slide toward it to restore balance. A value exactly equal to the mean adds zero distance, so the mean stays put — the same is true when you *remove* a value.

**Worked example:** 5 values with mean 12 (total 60). Add 18 → total 78, count 6, new mean 13. Add 12 instead → total 72 ÷ 6 = 12, unchanged.

**Try it yourself:** The mean of 7 numbers is 20. Will the mean rise, fall or stay the same if you remove the number 20? What if you remove 25?

## 3. Keep the mean fixed by adding balanced values
**Why it works:** Several new values leave the mean unchanged as long as *their own* average equals the old mean — their distances above and below cancel.

**Worked example:** Data with mean 8. Include 5 and 6 (3 and 2 below) plus one value above. To balance 3 + 2 = 5, that value must be 8 + 5 = 13. Check: (5 + 6 + 13) ÷ 3 = 8.

**Try it yourself:** A collection has mean 15. You include 12 and 20. Which one more value keeps the mean at 15?

## 4. Shift-and-scale shortcut (the shoe trick)
**Why it works:** Adding the same number k to every value adds exactly k to the mean (the extra k appears n times, and n·k ÷ n = k). Multiplying every value by m multiplies the mean by m. The dot plot just slides or stretches — the mean moves with it.

**Worked example:** 24 students measured with 1-cm shoes average 150.2 cm. No need to re-measure: correct mean = 150.2 − 1 = 149.2 cm. Doubling every value in a set with mean 7 gives mean 14; then adding 4 to each gives 18.

**Try it yourself:** A class's mean mark is 62. The teacher adds 5 bonus marks to everyone, then later realises each mark should be doubled for a 100-mark scale. What is the final mean?

## 5. Mean of evenly spaced numbers = (first + last) ÷ 2
**Why it works:** Numbers with equal gaps pair up symmetrically around the middle (1 with 50, 2 with 49, …), and every pair has the same total, so the mean is the midpoint of the extremes.

**Worked example:** First 50 natural numbers: (1 + 50) ÷ 2 = 25.5. First 50 odd numbers: (1 + 99) ÷ 2 = 50. First 50 multiples of 4: (4 + 200) ÷ 2 = 102.

**Try it yourself:** Find the mean of the first 30 multiples of 3 without adding them.

## 6. Missing value? Use total = mean × count
**Why it works:** Mean = total ÷ count, so the total is always mean × count. Subtract the values you know and the unknown pops out — you never need the individual values, only the total.

**Worked example:** 10 wrestlers average 39.2 kg → total 392 kg. The nine known weights add to 349, so the smudged one is 392 − 349 = 43 kg.

**Try it yourself:** The mean of 8, 13, 10, 4, 5, 20, y, 10 is 10.375. Find y.

## 7. Fixing an error in one value: divide the mistake by n
**Why it works:** If one value is recorded e too high, the total is e too high, so the mean is too high by e ÷ n — not by e.

**Worked example:** 15 trees, average 25.6 coconuts, one count noted 3 too high. Mean is too high by 3 ÷ 15 = 0.2, so the correct average is 25.4. (Long way: 25.6 × 15 = 384, minus 3 = 381, ÷ 15 = 25.4.)

**Try it yourself:** 20 marks have mean 41. One mark entered as 35 should have been 55. What is the correct mean?

## 8. Median from a frequency table: add the frequencies until you pass the middle
**Why it works:** Cumulative frequencies tell you which value sits at each sorted position, so you never have to write out the whole list.

**Worked example:** Family sizes 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 with frequencies 3, 11, 9, 7, 3, 1, 1, 1 (36 students). Median = average of the 18th and 19th values. Cumulative: 3 → 14 → 23. Positions 15–23 are all 5, so the median is 5. (And don't average the eight sizes alone — that gives a wrong 6.5; the real mean is 188 ÷ 36 ≈ 5.22.)

**Try it yourself:** Dart trials 1–10 with student counts 1, 0, 0, 1, 4, 9, 12, 15, 10, 10. Find the median number of trials.

## 9. Median tricks: find the middle *positions* first
**Why it works:** With n values the median is the middle position (odd n) or the average of the two middle positions (even n). Adding one value shifts those positions by half a step, so think about which values land there.

**Worked example:** 8, 10, 19, 23, 26, 34, 40, 41, 41, 48, 51, 55, 70, 84, 91, 92 — 16 values, median = (8th + 9th) ÷ 2 = (41 + 41) ÷ 2 = 41. Because *both* middle values are 41, adding any one number (or removing any one) still leaves a 41 in the middle!

**Try it yourself:** For the data 12, 47, 8, 73, 18, 35, 39, 8, 29, 25, p, which values of p keep the median at 29?

## 10. Reading a line graph: steeper means faster change — and don't over-read
**Why it works:** Connecting points in time order turns change into slope: a steeper segment means a bigger increase between those years. But a graph only shows what was measured — an average line says nothing about *every* individual, and lines for three countries say nothing about a fourth.

**Worked example:** Worldwide launches ≈ 2900 in 2023 and ≈ 2800 in 2024, so the count dipped — "it rose every year" is not a valid inference. "Nepal launched nothing" is also invalid: Nepal isn't plotted at all.

**Try it yourself:** A graph shows the average daily hobby time of rural kids by age. Someone says "every 15-year-old spends at least 1 hour on hobbies". What is wrong with that claim?
