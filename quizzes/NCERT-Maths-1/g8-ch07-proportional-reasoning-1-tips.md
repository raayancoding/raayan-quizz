# Proportional Reasoning-1 — Tips & Tricks (Grade 8, NCERT Ganita Prakash Ch. 7)

Quick tricks pulled straight from the chapter. Each one tells you **why** it works, shows a worked example, and leaves you one to try.

## 1. Multiply, don't add — the "same factor" test

**The trick:** Two ratios are proportional only if *both* terms change by the **same multiplying factor**. Adding or subtracting the same number to both terms breaks the ratio.

**Why it works:** A ratio compares by division (how many of one for every so many of the other). Dividing 30 by 60 and 20 by 40 both give ½ — the shape stays the same. Subtracting 20 from 60 and 40 gives 40 and 20: 40/60 = 2/3 but 20/40 = 1/2 — different factors, so the picture gets stretched (that is why tiger image B looked elongated).

**Worked example:** Neelima is 3, her mother is 30 → ratio 1 : 10. Nine years later they are 12 and 39 → 12 : 39 = 4 : 13. Adding 9 to both changed the ratio.

**Try it yourself:** A photo is 80 mm × 50 mm. Which keeps it similar: making it 40 mm × 25 mm, or making it 60 mm × 30 mm?

## 2. Shrink to simplest form with the HCF

**The trick:** Divide both terms by their **HCF** to get the simplest form. Compare simplest forms to see if two ratios are proportional.

**Why it works:** Dividing both terms by the same number is the same as multiplying by a fraction — the ratio does not change. The HCF gets you to the smallest whole-number terms in one step.

**Worked example:** 72 : 96 → HCF of 72 and 96 is 24 → 72 ÷ 24 : 96 ÷ 24 = 3 : 4. So 3 : 4 :: 72 : 96.

**Try it yourself:** Write 45 : 60 in simplest form. Is it proportional to 6 : 8?

## 3. The cross-multiplication check (ad = bc)

**The trick:** For a : b :: c : d, multiply the outside pair and the inside pair. If **a × d = b × c**, the proportion is true.

**Why it works:** If c = f × a and d = f × b (same factor f), then c/a = d/b. Multiply both sides by ab and you get bc = ad. If the cross products differ, the factors differ.

**Worked example:** Is 21 : 6 :: 35 : 10 true? 21 × 10 = 210 and 6 × 35 = 210 — yes! Is 8 : 3 :: 24 : 6 true? 8 × 6 = 48 but 3 × 24 = 72 — no.

**Try it yourself:** Check 12 : 18 :: 28 : 12 with cross products.

## 4. Find the factor of change first

**The trick:** When one term is known in both ratios, divide the new term by the old one to get the **factor**, then multiply the other term by that factor.

**Why it works:** Both terms must change by the same factor. Once you know it from one term, the other term just follows.

**Worked example:** 6 glasses of lemonade need 10 spoons of sugar. For 18 glasses: factor = 18 ÷ 6 = 3, so sugar = 10 × 3 = 30 spoons. The factor can be a fraction too: 14 : 21 :: 6 : ? → factor 6/14 = 3/7, so ? = 21 × 3/7 = 9.

**Try it yourself:** 18 : 24 :: 30 : ? (Hint: find the factor from 18 → 30.)

## 5. Āryabhaṭa's Rule of Three

**The trick:** With three known quantities, find the fourth with
**ichchhāphala = (phala × ichchhā) ÷ pramāṇa**, i.e. d = (b × c) ÷ a.

**Why it works:** It is just cross-multiplication rearranged: ad = bc, so d = bc/a. Ancient Indian mathematicians used it to solve exactly the same proportion problems.

**Worked example:** 120 students need 15 kg of rice. For 80 students: d = 15 × 80 ÷ 120 = 10 kg.

**Try it yourself:** A car covers 90 km in 150 minutes. Set up the Rule of Three to find the distance in 300 minutes.

## 6. Match the units before you proportion

**The trick:** Make sure both ratios use the **same unit** for the same kind of quantity — minutes with minutes, grams with grams — before cross-multiplying.

**Why it works:** 150 : 90 :: 4 : ? mixes minutes and hours, so the "factor" means nothing. Converting 4 h to 240 min first gives 150 : 90 :: 240 : 144.

**Worked example:** Himachal tea: 200 g for ₹200 (1 : 1). Meghalaya tea: 1 kg for ₹800. Convert 1 kg to 1000 g → 1000 : 800 = 5 : 4. Not proportional — and 1 kg of Himachal tea would cost ₹1,000, so it is the pricier tea.

**Try it yourself:** A tap fills a 500 mL mug in 15 seconds. Convert 10 litres to millilitres, then find the time for the bucket.

## 7. Spot the "upside-down" problems

**The trick:** Before using the Rule of Three ask: *when one quantity goes up, does the other go up too?* If the other goes **down** (faster speed → less time, faster tractor → fewer hours), the Rule of Three does **not** apply.

**Why it works:** Proportion means both terms grow by the same factor. In speed–time problems the product (distance) stays fixed instead, so one quantity shrinks as the other grows.

**Worked example:** 2 hours at 50 km/h is 100 km. At 75 km/h the time is 100 ÷ 75 = 1⅓ h = 80 minutes — less, not more. Writing 50 : 2 :: 75 : 3 would wrongly say 3 hours.

**Try it yourself:** A pair of oxen takes 6 hours per acre. A tractor is 4 times faster. Does the tractor take 24 hours or 1.5 hours per acre — and why?

## 8. Share in a ratio: count the groups

**The trick:** To split x in the ratio m : n, count the groups (m + n), find the group size x ÷ (m + n), then multiply: first part = m × group size, second = n × group size.

**Why it works:** The ratio tells you how many equal groups each person gets, not how many objects. Dealing 3-and-1 repeatedly until 12 counters run out gives 9 and 3 — the shortcut just does all the rounds at once.

**Worked example:** 42 counters in the ratio 4 : 3 → 7 groups of 42 ÷ 7 = 6 → 4 × 6 = 24 and 3 × 6 = 18. Check: 24 + 18 = 42 and 24 : 18 = 4 : 3.

**Try it yourself:** Divide ₹6,000 in the ratio 2 : 3. (Check that the parts add back to ₹6,000.)

## 9. Changing a mixture: hold the untouched part fixed

**The trick:** When only one ingredient is added, the other ingredient's amount **stays the same**. Use it as the anchor for the new ratio.

**Why it works:** If you only pour in cement, the sand is unchanged. Write the new ratio with the sand amount, find the new cement amount, then subtract what you already had.

**Worked example:** 40 kg of 3 : 1 sand–cement has 30 kg sand and 10 kg cement. For 5 : 2 with 30 kg sand, cement = 2/5 × 30 = 12 kg, so add 12 − 10 = 2 kg.

**Try it yourself:** 40 mL of green paint is blue : yellow = 3 : 5. If 20 mL of blue is added, what is the new ratio?

## 10. Temperature is the odd one out

**The trick:** Most conversions (metres → feet, litres → mL) are plain multiplications, but °C → °F needs **multiply by 9/5 and then add 32**. Going back: subtract 32 first, then multiply by 5/9.

**Why it works:** 0 °C is 32 °F, not 0 °F, so the scales are shifted. Because of the +32, doubling °C does not double °F.

**Worked example:** 25 °C → 9/5 × 25 = 45 → 45 + 32 = 77 °F. Back: 77 − 32 = 45 → 5/9 × 45 = 25 °C.

**Try it yourself:** Convert 35 °C to °F, then convert 68 °F to °C.
