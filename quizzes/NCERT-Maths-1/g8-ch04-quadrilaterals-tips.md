# Quadrilaterals — Tips & Tricks (NCERT Grade 8, Ganita Prakash Ch. 4)

Nine quick tricks pulled straight from the chapter. Each one tells you *why* it works, shows a worked example, and leaves you a "Try it yourself" to test on your own.

## 1. The 360° Rule — split it into two triangles

**The trick:** The four angles of ANY quadrilateral add up to 360°. Missing one angle? Add the other three and subtract from 360.

**Why it works:** Draw one diagonal. The quadrilateral becomes two triangles, and each triangle's angles add to 180°. 180° + 180° = 360°. This even works for a "dented" (concave) quadrilateral — exercise Q10 proves it the same way.

**Worked example:** Angles are 70°, 110° and 85°. 70 + 110 + 85 = 265. Fourth angle = 360 − 265 = **95°**.

**Try it yourself:** A quadrilateral has angles 48°, 132° and 97°. Find the fourth angle.

## 2. "Three right angles means four" — the automatic rectangle

**The trick:** If a quadrilateral has three 90° angles, the fourth is forced to be 90° too, so it is a rectangle. You never need to measure the fourth corner.

**Why it works:** 90 + 90 + 90 = 270, and 360 − 270 = 90. There is no room for anything else. Exercise Q11(ii) is True for exactly this reason.

**Worked example:** A window frame has three corners measured at 90°. Is the frame a rectangle? Yes — the fourth corner must be 360 − 270 = 90°, and "all angles 90°" is the chapter's shortest definition of a rectangle.

**Try it yourself:** A quadrilateral has angles 90°, 90° and 100°. Could it be a rectangle? What must the fourth angle be?

## 3. Diagonals tell you the shape — the "equal / bisect / perpendicular" checklist

**The trick:** Look only at the two diagonals and tick three boxes:

| Bisect each other? | Equal? | Perpendicular? | Shape |
|---|---|---|---|
| ✓ | ✗ | ✗ | Parallelogram |
| ✓ | ✓ | ✗ | Rectangle |
| ✓ | ✗ | ✓ | Rhombus |
| ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | Square |
| one bisects the other | ✗ | ✓ | Kite |

**Why it works:** This is the Carpenter's Problem. Equal diagonals that cross at their midpoints force every corner to be (90° − x/2) + x/2 = 90°, whatever the angle x between them — so you get a rectangle. Add "perpendicular" and the sides become equal too (Deduction 5): a square. Drop "equal" but keep "perpendicular" and you get a rhombus (Deduction 10).

**Worked example:** Two 8 cm sticks crossed at their midpoints at 40° → equal + bisect → **rectangle**. The same sticks crossed at 90° → **square**. One stick stretched to 12 cm, still crossing the 8 cm stick at its midpoint at 90° → bisect + perpendicular, not equal → **rhombus**.

**Try it yourself:** Diagonals of 7 cm and 5 cm cross at their midpoints at 140°. Which shape is it? What if they crossed at 90° instead?

## 4. Parallelogram angles — "one angle gives all four"

**The trick:** In a parallelogram (and so in a rectangle, rhombus or square), knowing ONE angle x gives the rest instantly: opposite angle = x, both neighbours = 180° − x.

**Why it works:** Opposite sides are parallel, so a side acts as a transversal. Neighbouring angles are co-interior angles and add to 180° (Deduction 6). Doing this twice brings you back to x for the opposite corner.

**Worked example:** Parallelogram with ∠A = 30°. ∠D = 180 − 30 = **150°**, ∠B = **150°**, ∠C = **30°**. Check: 30 + 150 + 30 + 150 = 360 ✓.

**Try it yourself:** A parallelogram has one angle of 72°. Write down the other three angles.

## 5. Rhombus half-angle trick — the diagonal cuts the corner in half

**The trick:** In a rhombus the diagonal bisects the corner angle. So if a diagonal makes an angle *a* with a side, the whole corner is 2a, and the neighbouring corner is 180° − 2a.

**Why it works:** A diagonal splits the rhombus into two isosceles triangles with all sides equal, so the triangles are congruent (SSS) and the four angles at the diagonal's ends are equal (Deduction 9). This is Property 5 of a rhombus.

**Worked example:** In rhombus UVWX, diagonal VX makes 30° with side UV. Corner V = 2 × 30 = **60°**. Corner U = 180 − 60 = **120°**. The chapter's rhombus with a 50° corner works the other way: half-angle a satisfies a + a + 50 = 180, so a = **65°**.

**Try it yourself:** A diagonal of a rhombus makes 20° with one side. Find all four angles of the rhombus.

## 6. Rectangle diagonal angles — look for the isosceles triangles

**The trick:** When the diagonals of a rectangle cross at O, you get four isosceles triangles (OA = OB = OC = OD). Given the angle at O, each base angle is (180° − that angle) ÷ 2.

**Why it works:** The diagonals of a rectangle are equal AND bisect each other (Property 4), so all four half-diagonals are the same length. Equal sides mean equal base angles. Vertically opposite angles at O are equal and neighbours at O add to 180°.

**Worked example:** Rectangle PQRS, ∠POS = 110°. Then ∠QOR = 110° (vertically opposite), so ∠OQR = ∠ORQ = (180 − 110) ÷ 2 = **35°**. ∠POQ = 180 − 110 = 70°, so ∠OPQ = ∠OQP = (180 − 70) ÷ 2 = **55°**. Check at corner Q: 35 + 55 = 90 ✓.

**Try it yourself:** In a rectangle the diagonals cross at 80°. Find the two different base angles of the four triangles, and check they add to 90°.

## 7. Trapezium angles — pair them along the "legs"

**The trick:** In a trapezium with PQ ∥ SR, pair each angle with the one at the OTHER end of the same non-parallel side (leg): ∠P + ∠S = 180° and ∠Q + ∠R = 180°. Don't pair angles along a parallel side — they need not add to 180°.

**Why it works:** Each leg is a transversal between the two parallel sides, so the two angles at its ends are co-interior angles (Property 1 of a trapezium). For an isosceles trapezium there is a bonus: the two angles at the same parallel side are equal (Property 2).

**Worked example:** PQ ∥ RS, ∠P = 135°, ∠Q = 105°. ∠S = 180 − 135 = **45°**, ∠R = 180 − 105 = **75°**. Isosceles trapezium with AD = BC and ∠D = 100°: ∠A = 80°, and ∠B = ∠A = **80°**, so ∠C = 100°.

**Try it yourself:** Trapezium ABCD has AB ∥ DC, ∠A = 115° and ∠B = 70°. Find ∠C and ∠D.

## 8. The "is it also a …?" ladder — Venn thinking

**The trick:** Shapes sit inside each other like nested boxes. Going DOWN the ladder always works; going UP needs extra checks.

```
quadrilateral
 └─ parallelogram (opposite sides parallel)
     ├─ rectangle (all angles 90°)
     ├─ rhombus (all sides equal)  ← also a kite
     └─ square = rectangle AND rhombus
```

**Why it works:** A square satisfies every definition above it, just like the chapter's "I am an Indian and I am a Malayali" cartoon — belonging to the small set does not stop you belonging to the big one. But a parallelogram is NOT automatically a rectangle, and a kite is NOT automatically a rhombus (6, 6, 9, 9 is a kite with no equal opposite sides).

**Worked example:** "Every square is a rhombus" → True (all sides equal). "Every rhombus is a square" → False (angles may be 60° and 120°). "Every rhombus is a kite" → True (adjacent sides equal). "Every kite is a rhombus" → False.

**Try it yourself:** Decide True or False: (a) every rectangle is a parallelogram; (b) every parallelogram is a rhombus; (c) a quadrilateral that is both a rectangle and a rhombus must be a square.

## 9. Spot the congruence condition — "what do the two triangles share?"

**The trick:** Before proving a property, ask: what is shared (a common side or vertically opposite angles), what is given (equal sides, right angles), and what comes from parallel lines (alternate or co-interior angles)? Then name the condition: SSS, SAS, ASA or AAS. And write the vertices in matching order!

**Why it works:** Every property in the chapter comes from a congruence: equal diagonals of a rectangle (SAS — Deduction 1), diagonals bisecting (AAS for a rectangle, ASA for a parallelogram), square diagonals at 90° (SSS — Deduction 5). Once two triangles are congruent, their corresponding parts are equal — that's where the property pops out.

**Worked example:** Rectangle ABCD, show AC = BD. ΔADC and ΔDAB: AB = CD (given), ∠BAD = ∠CDA = 90° (given, and it is the INCLUDED angle), AD common. So **SAS**, and AC = BD. Writing ΔADC ≅ ΔDAB is right; ΔADC ≅ ΔABD would pair the wrong vertices.

**Try it yourself:** In parallelogram ABCD with diagonal BD, which pairs of equal angles come from the parallel sides, and which side is common? Which congruence condition (SSS, SAS, ASA, AAS) proves ΔABD ≅ ΔCDB?
