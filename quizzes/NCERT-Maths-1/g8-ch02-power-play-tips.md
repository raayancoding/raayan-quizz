# Power Play — Tips and Tricks for Smart Kids! ⚡📐

Hi Grade 8 mathematician! This chapter is all about **powers** — numbers that grow by
*multiplying* again and again instead of adding. A sheet of paper folded 46 times
reaches the Moon; a ladder with 20 cm steps needs nearly two billion rungs to do the
same job. The tricks below help you tame exponents, zero and negative powers,
scientific notation and the giant numbers of the real world. Read each trick, follow
the worked example with your pencil, then try the mini practice yourself.

---

## Trick 1: Count the Doublings — Every 10 Folds Means "× 1024"

**Why it works:** Folding doubles the thickness, so after *n* folds the thickness is
multiplied by 2ⁿ. The key landmark is 2¹⁰ = 1024 ≈ 1000. So every 10 folds (or 10
doublings of anything) multiplies by roughly a thousand — that is three extra zeros.
Any time you see repeated doubling, think in blocks of ten.

**Worked example:** A 0.001 cm sheet after 30 folds.
1. 30 folds = three blocks of 10 folds.
2. Each block multiplies by 1024 ≈ 1000, so about 1000 × 1000 × 1000 = 10⁹ times thicker.
3. 0.001 cm × 10⁹ = 10⁶ cm = 10 km — the chapter's exact value is 10.7 km. Planes fly
   at about that height!

**Try it yourself:** Using the same landmark, estimate how thick the sheet is after
40 folds (in km). Is it bigger than the Earth's diameter of about 12,700 km?

## Trick 2: Same Base? Add the Exponents (Multiply) or Subtract Them (Divide)

**Why it works:** 3⁴ × 3³ means (3 × 3 × 3 × 3) × (3 × 3 × 3) — seven 3s in a row, so
it is 3⁷. Counting the factors is the same as adding 4 + 3. Dividing removes factors,
so 2¹⁰⁰ ÷ 2²⁵ leaves 100 − 25 = 75 twos. The base **never** changes.

**Worked example:** Find 2⁹ without a calculator.
1. Split the exponent: 9 = 4 + 5, so 2⁹ = 2⁴ × 2⁵.
2. 2⁴ = 16 and 2⁵ = 32.
3. 16 × 32 = 512. ✓ (Check: 2¹⁰ = 1024, and half of that is 512.)

**Try it yourself:** Simplify 7⁵ × 7⁻² ÷ 7 and write the answer as a single power of 7.

---

## Trick 3: Power of a Power — MULTIPLY the Exponents

**Why it works:** (4³)² means 4³ × 4³, and by Trick 2 that is 4³⁺³ = 4⁶. Two copies of
"three 4s" is 3 × 2 = 6 fours. Mixing this up with Trick 2 (adding) is the most common
exponent mistake in the chapter — and the quiz tests it!

**Worked example:** Write 8⁶ as a power of 2.
1. 8 = 2³, so 8⁶ = (2³)⁶.
2. Multiply the exponents: 3 × 6 = 18.
3. 8⁶ = 2¹⁸. (Adding would wrongly give 2⁹ = 512, but 8⁶ is 2,62,144.)

**Try it yourself:** Write 9¹⁴ as a power of 3 in two different ways: first as
(9²)⁷ → …, then using 9 = 3².

---

## Trick 4: Same Exponent, Different Bases? Multiply the Bases

**Why it works:** 2⁴ × 3⁴ = (2 × 2 × 2 × 2) × (3 × 3 × 3 × 3). Pair each 2 with a 3 to
get (2 × 3) × (2 × 3) × (2 × 3) × (2 × 3) = 6⁴. The rule mᵃ × nᵃ = (mn)ᵃ turns two hard
multiplications into one easy one — and it works for division too: 10⁴ ÷ 5⁴ = 2⁴.

**Worked example:** Find 2⁵ × 5⁵.
1. Same exponent (5), so multiply the bases: 2 × 5 = 10.
2. 2⁵ × 5⁵ = 10⁵ = 1,00,000. No need to compute 32 × 3125!

**Try it yourself:** Use the trick to find 4³ × 25³ in your head.

---

## Trick 5: Zero and Negative Exponents — Keep the Pattern Going

**Why it works:** Look at the power line of 4: 64, 16, 4 … each step to the right
*divides* by 4. Continue the pattern: 4¹ = 4, so 4⁰ = 4 ÷ 4 = 1, then 4⁻¹ = 1 ÷ 4 = 1/4,
then 4⁻² = 1/16. A negative exponent means "one over the positive power" — it never
makes a number negative, and anything (except 0) to the power 0 is 1.

**Worked example:** Simplify 2⁴ ÷ 2⁵.
1. Subtract exponents: 4 − 5 = −1, so the answer is 2⁻¹.
2. Expand to check: 16 ÷ 32 = 1/2. So 2⁻¹ = 1/2, not −2.

**Try it yourself:** Write 10⁻³ as a fraction and as a decimal, then say what 7⁰ × 7⁻²
equals.

## Trick 6: The Exponent Is the Boss in Scientific Notation

**Why it works:** In x × 10ʸ the exponent y fixes how many digits the number has; the
coefficient x only fine-tunes. Changing 2 × 10⁷ to 3 × 10⁷ makes the number 1.5 times
bigger, but changing it to 2 × 10⁸ makes it 10 times bigger. So to compare giant
numbers, compare exponents **first** and look at coefficients only if they tie.

**Worked example:** Which is smaller, Sun–Earth 1.496 × 10¹¹ m or Sun–Saturn
1.4335 × 10¹² m?
1. Exponents: 11 vs 12 — different, so stop here.
2. 10¹¹ is ten times smaller than 10¹², so Sun–Earth is the smaller distance even
   though 1.496 is bigger than 1.4335.

**Try it yourself:** Put these in order from smallest to largest: 9.8 × 10⁵,
1.2 × 10⁷, 3.3 × 10⁶, 9.9 × 10⁶.

## Trick 7: Converting to Standard Form — Count the Digit Hops

**Why it works:** Standard form needs a coefficient between 1 and 10. Hop the decimal
point until only one non-zero digit is in front of it; the number of hops is the
exponent (positive if you hopped left, negative if you hopped right). A whole number
with *d* digits always gets exponent *d* − 1.

**Worked example:** Write 34,30,000 in standard form.
1. 34,30,000 has 7 digits, so the exponent will be 7 − 1 = 6.
2. Hop the point 6 places left: 3.43.
3. Answer: 3.43 × 10⁶. (Keep inner zeros: 70,04,00,00,000 becomes 7.004 × 10¹⁰, not
   7.4 × 10¹⁰.)

**Try it yourself:** Write 59,853 and 0.00072 in standard form.

---

## Trick 8: Multiplying Giants — Coefficients Together, Exponents Added

**Why it works:** (a × 10ᵐ) × (b × 10ⁿ) = (a × b) × 10ᵐ⁺ⁿ because of Trick 2. Then, if
a × b is 10 or more, hop the point once more and add 1 to the exponent so the answer
is back in standard form.

**Worked example:** 38 trillion bacteria per person × 8.2 × 10⁹ people.
1. 38 trillion = 38 × 10¹². Coefficients: 38 × 8.2 = 311.6. Exponents: 12 + 9 = 21.
2. 311.6 × 10²¹ is not standard form — hop the point two places: 3.116, and add 2 to
   the exponent.
3. Total: 3.116 × 10²³ bacterial cells.

**Try it yourself:** 10⁸ bee colonies with 50,000 bees each — how many bees? Write the
answer in standard form.

## Trick 9: Choices Multiply — Count Combinations with Powers

**Why it works:** If each slot of a lock has 10 choices and the slots are independent,
every password for the first slot pairs with every choice for the second, and so on.
Two slots give 10 × 10 = 100, three give 1000 … n slots give 10ⁿ. Never add the
choices — a 5-digit lock has 10⁵ = 1,00,000 passwords, not 50.

**Worked example:** A 5-character locker code using letters A–Z or digits 0–9.
1. Choices per slot: 26 letters + 10 digits = 36.
2. Five independent slots: 36 × 36 × 36 × 36 × 36 = 36⁵.
3. That is about 6 crore codes — far safer than the 10⁵ of a digits-only lock.

**Try it yourself:** How many 6-letter passwords (A–Z only) are possible? Write it as a
power. Which is bigger: that, or a 10-digit numeric code?

## Trick 10: Guess, Then Estimate — Tulābhāra Thinking

**Why it works:** For questions like "how many 1-rupee coins weigh as much as me?" you
rarely have exact data. The chapter's method: make a quick **guess**, write the
**relationship** between the quantities, make **reasonable assumptions** for the
unknowns, **compute**, then **compare** with your guess. Different sensible assumptions
give different answers — and that is perfectly fine. Powers of 10 keep the estimate
tidy.

**Worked example:** How long would a 400 km pādayātra take?
1. Guess: a couple of weeks?
2. Relationship: days = distance ÷ distance walked per day.
3. Assumption: about 40 km a day.
4. Compute: 400 ÷ 40 = 10 days. Compare: close to the guess — good.

**Try it yourself:** Estimate how many breaths you have taken in your life so far.
Write your assumptions (breaths per minute, your age) and give the answer in standard
form.

### Quick reminder card

| Situation | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Same base, multiply | add exponents | 3⁴ × 3³ = 3⁷ |
| Same base, divide | subtract exponents | 2¹⁰⁰ ÷ 2²⁵ = 2⁷⁵ |
| Power of a power | multiply exponents | (4³)² = 4⁶ |
| Same exponent, multiply | multiply bases | 2⁵ × 5⁵ = 10⁵ |
| Zero exponent | equals 1 (base ≠ 0) | 7⁰ = 1 |
| Negative exponent | one over the power | 2⁻⁶ = 1/64 |
| Standard form | 1 ≤ x < 10, times 10ʸ | 5900 = 5.9 × 10³ |
| Indian names | ×100 each step | lakh 10⁵, crore 10⁷, arab 10⁹, kharab 10¹¹ |
| International names | ×1000 each step | million 10⁶, billion 10⁹, trillion 10¹² |
