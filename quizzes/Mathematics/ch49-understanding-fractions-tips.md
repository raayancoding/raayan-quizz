# Understanding Fractions — Tips and Tricks for Smart Kids! 🍕➗

Hi Grade 4 explorer! Fractions are just a way of talking about PARTS of
something — a chikki, a pizza, a set of cars, or a number line. The tricks
below come straight from your chapter. Read each one, follow the worked
example with your pencil, and then try the mini practice yourself!

---

## Trick 1: Top = Taken, Bottom = Total

**Why it works:** Every fraction has two numbers separated by the fraction bar
(the book calls it a *vinculum*). The **denominator** at the bottom tells you
how many EQUAL parts the whole was cut into (the *total*). The **numerator** on
top tells you how many of those parts you are talking about (the *taken*). Once
you remember "top = taken, bottom = total", you can read any fraction.

**Worked example:** A chikki is cut into 8 equal triangles and you eat 2.
1. Total equal pieces → bottom: 8
2. Pieces taken (eaten) → top: 2
3. Fraction eaten = 2/8, read "two eighths".

**Try it yourself:** A chocolate bar has 9 equal squares and 4 are eaten. Write
the fraction eaten and say its word name.

---

## Trick 2: The "What's Left?" Trick — the Whole is n/n

**Why it works:** If a whole is cut into 6 equal parts, ALL six parts together
make the whole, so the whole can be written as 6/6. That means the unshaded
(or uneaten) part is simply *total minus taken*, written over the SAME bottom
number. The denominator never changes — only the top does.

**Worked example:** 4/6 of a rectangle is shaded. What fraction is unshaded?
1. Write the whole with the same bottom: 6/6
2. Subtract the tops only: 6 − 4 = 2
3. Unshaded = 2/6. (Check: 4/6 + 2/6 = 6/6 = one whole ✓)

**Try it yourself:** 2/5 of a shape is shaded. What fraction is unshaded?

---

## Trick 3: Fraction of a Set — Divide by the Bottom, Multiply by the Top

**Why it works:** A fraction can also describe part of a GROUP, like 12 cars.
"1/4 of 12" means split 12 into 4 equal groups, so 12 ÷ 4 = 3 cars in each
group. "3/4 of 12" means take 3 of those groups: 3 × 3 = 9. So: divide by the
denominator to find ONE part, then multiply by the numerator to find how many
parts you want.

**Worked example:** Find 2/3 of 12 cars.
1. Divide by the bottom: 12 ÷ 3 = 4 (that is 1/3 of the cars)
2. Multiply by the top: 2 × 4 = 8
3. So 2/3 of 12 cars = 8 cars.

**Try it yourself:** There are 18 roses. How many roses is 2/3 of them?

---

## Trick 4: Proper or Improper? Just Compare Top and Bottom

**Why it works:** A **proper** fraction is less than one whole, so its top is
SMALLER than its bottom (3/4, 1/2, 9/11). An **improper** fraction is equal to
or MORE than a whole, so its top is bigger than or equal to its bottom (7/4,
5/2, 9/9). A **mixed** fraction is a whole number plus a proper fraction
(1 1/2 is "one and a half"). And a **unit** fraction always has 1 on top — so
it is always proper!

**Worked example:** Sort 4/7, 9/6, 1/5, 10/7, 9/2.
1. Top smaller than bottom → proper: 4/7, 1/5
2. Top bigger than bottom → improper: 9/6, 10/7, 9/2
3. Any with 1 on top? 1/5 is also a unit fraction.

**Try it yourself:** Which of 5/8, 7/7, 13/9, 1/9 are proper, which are
improper, and which is a unit fraction?

---

## Trick 5: Improper → Mixed with Short Division (Q R/D)

**Why it works:** 7/3 means seven one-thirds. Every 3 one-thirds make a whole,
so dividing 7 by 3 tells you how many wholes you have (the **quotient**) and
how many thirds are left over (the **remainder**). The book writes this as
"Q R/D": Quotient, then Remainder over Divisor. The denominator stays the same.

**Worked example:** Convert 13/4 to a mixed fraction.
1. Divide: 13 ÷ 4 → 4 × 3 = 12, so quotient = 3
2. Remainder: 13 − 12 = 1
3. Whole part = 3, new top = 1, bottom stays 4 → 13/4 = 3 1/4.

**Try it yourself:** Convert 27/5 to a mixed fraction using Q R/D.

---

## Trick 6: Mixed → Improper: "Multiply, Add, Keep"

**Why it works:** In 2 3/4, the 2 wholes are each worth 4 quarters, so they
hold 2 × 4 = 8 quarters. Add the 3 extra quarters and you have 11 quarters in
all: 11/4. So **multiply** the whole by the bottom, **add** the top, and
**keep** the bottom. Watch out: adding the whole and the top first (2 + 3 = 5)
is the classic mistake!

**Worked example:** Convert 5 1/3 to an improper fraction.
1. Multiply: 5 × 3 = 15
2. Add: 15 + 1 = 16
3. Keep the bottom: 5 1/3 = 16/3.

**Try it yourself:** Convert 4 3/11 to an improper fraction with
"multiply, add, keep".

---

## Trick 7: Equivalent Fractions — Same Number, Top AND Bottom

**Why it works:** Fold a strip in half and colour one half: 1/2. Fold it again
and the same colour now shows as 2/4; fold once more and it is 4/8. The
coloured part never changed — only the number of pieces did. So multiplying or
dividing BOTH the top and bottom by the SAME non-zero number gives an
equivalent fraction. Doing it to only one of them breaks the fraction.

**Worked example:** Find three fractions equivalent to 2/5.
1. × 2: (2 × 2)/(5 × 2) = 4/10
2. × 3: (2 × 3)/(5 × 3) = 6/15
3. × 4: (2 × 4)/(5 × 4) = 8/20
So 2/5 = 4/10 = 6/15 = 8/20. To go the other way, 50/60 ÷ 10 on top and bottom
gives 5/6.

**Try it yourself:** Fill the blank: 3/8 = 9/? Then write 20/24 with a smaller
top and bottom by dividing both by 4.

## Trick 8: Comparing Like Fractions — Same Bottom, Just Compare Tops

**Why it works:** Like fractions have the SAME denominator, so every piece is
the same size. More pieces means more chocolate! That is why 5/6 > 2/6: five
sixths beat two sixths. On a number line the greater fraction is always the one
farther to the RIGHT. (Careful — this only works when the bottoms match.)

**Worked example:** Arrange 9/12, 2/12 and 5/12 in ascending order.
1. Same bottom (12), so look only at the tops: 9, 2, 5
2. Order the tops smallest to biggest: 2 < 5 < 9
3. Ascending order: 2/12, 5/12, 9/12.

**Try it yourself:** Arrange 7/18, 12/18 and 9/18 in descending order.

---

## Trick 9: The Unit-Fraction Surprise — Bigger Bottom, Smaller Piece

**Why it works:** Imagine ONE chocolate bar. Cut it into 2 pieces and each piece
is huge (1/2). Cut it into 6 pieces and each piece is tiny (1/6). So for unit
fractions, the bigger the denominator, the SMALLER the share — the opposite of
what the big number makes you expect!

**Worked example:** Which is the biggest share of the same bar: 1/2, 1/3 or 1/6?
1. 2 pieces → each piece is big
2. 3 pieces → each piece is smaller
3. 6 pieces → each piece is smallest
So 1/2 > 1/3 > 1/6, and 1/2 is the biggest share.

**Try it yourself:** Would you rather have 1/4 or 1/8 of the same pizza? Which
one is more, and why?

---

## Trick 10: Adding and Subtracting Like Fractions — Tops Only!

**Why it works:** 1/6 + 3/6 means one sixth-piece plus three sixth-pieces, which
is four sixth-pieces: 4/6. The pieces are still sixths, so the denominator does
NOT change. Adding the bottoms (to get 4/12) would say the pieces shrank, which
is wrong. The same rule works for subtraction: 9/16 − 2/16 = 7/16. For "how
much is left" problems, write the whole as n/n first (Trick 2) and subtract.

**Worked example:** Anita ate 2/7 of a cake and Rohit ate 4/7. How much is left?
1. Add the tops for the eaten part: 2 + 4 = 6 → 6/7 eaten
2. Write the whole cake as 7/7
3. Subtract the tops: 7 − 6 = 1 → 1/7 of the cake is left.

**Try it yourself:** Manan spends 5/15 of his pocket money on a card and 7/15 on
a gift. What fraction did he spend in all, and what fraction is left?

---

### Quick Reminder Card

- **Top = taken, bottom = total.** Parts must be EQUAL.
- **Whole = n/n** (2/2, 6/6, 20/20). Use it for "what's left" questions.
- **Fraction of a set:** divide by the bottom, multiply by the top.
- **Proper** top < bottom · **Improper** top ≥ bottom · **Mixed** whole + proper
  part · **Unit** 1 on top.
- **Improper → Mixed:** divide; quotient is the whole, remainder goes on top.
- **Mixed → Improper:** multiply, add, keep.
- **Equivalent:** × or ÷ the SAME number on top and bottom.
- **Like fractions:** compare, add or subtract the TOPS only — the bottom stays.
- **Unit fractions:** bigger bottom = smaller piece.
