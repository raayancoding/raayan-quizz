# Factors, Multiples and Divisibility — Tips and Tricks for Smart Kids! 🔢✨

Hi Grade 4 mathematician! This chapter asks one big question: *what makes things
fit perfectly into groups?* Once you know a few tricks about factors, multiples,
primes and divisibility, you can answer "does it divide exactly?" in seconds —
often without doing any long division at all. Read each trick, follow the worked
example with your pencil, and then try the mini practice yourself.

---

## Trick 1: Find Factors in PAIRS — and Stop When They Repeat

**Why it works:** Every factor has a partner that multiplies with it to make the
number. Think of arrays (rectangles): 6 blocks can be 1 row of 6 or 2 rows of 3.
So factors always come in pairs. As you test 1, 2, 3, 4 … the partner gets smaller
and smaller. When the two numbers in a pair swap places (like 3 × 4 becoming
4 × 3), you have found them all — stop!

**Worked example:** Find all the factors of 24.
1. 1 × 24 = 24 → pair (1, 24)
2. 2 × 12 = 24 → pair (2, 12)
3. 3 × 8 = 24 → pair (3, 8)
4. 4 × 6 = 24 → pair (4, 6)
5. 5? 24 ÷ 5 is not exact — skip.
6. 6 × 4 … that is pair (4, 6) again — the factors have started repeating, so stop.
7. Collect them in order: **1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24** — eight factors.

**Try it yourself:** Use factor pairs to list all the factors of 36. (Watch out —
one pair will have the same number twice!)

## Trick 2: The Remainder Test — Is It a Factor or Not?

**Why it works:** Multiplication and division are inverse operations. If a number
divides another number *exactly* (remainder 0), it is a factor and the quotient
is its partner factor. If there is any remainder at all, it is not a factor. You
never have to guess — just divide and look at the remainder.

**Worked example:** Is 6 a factor of 52?
1. 6 × 8 = 48, which is the biggest multiple of 6 that fits under 52.
2. 52 − 48 = 4, so 52 ÷ 6 = 8 **remainder 4**.
3. The remainder is not 0, so 6 is **not** a factor of 52.
4. Compare: 65 ÷ 5 = 13 remainder 0 — so 5 **is** a factor of 65, and (5, 13)
   is a factor pair.

**Try it yourself:** A baker has 42 cupcakes. Use the remainder test to decide
whether he can arrange them in groups of 6, and then in groups of 8.

## Trick 3: Multiples Live in the Times Table (and Never End)

**Why it works:** A multiple of a number is what you get when you multiply it by
1, 2, 3, 4 … — so the multiples of 6 are exactly the 6 times table: 6, 12, 18, 24,
30 … Counting numbers never end, so multiples never end either. That gives you two
facts you can always rely on: the **least** multiple of a number is the number
itself, and there is **no greatest** multiple.

**Worked example:** Every 4th house in a row of 20 houses is painted yellow.
Which houses are yellow, and how many are there?
1. "Every 4th" means the house numbers are multiples of 4.
2. Write the 4 times table up to 20: 4, 8, 12, 16, 20.
3. That is 5 houses — the same as 20 ÷ 4 = 5.

**Try it yourself:** An orchard has 50 trees and every 5th tree is an apple
tree. List the apple-tree numbers and say how many there are.

## Trick 4: Factors vs Multiples — The "Smaller or Bigger" Memory Hook

**Why it works:** Students often mix up factors and multiples. Here is the hook:
**factors fit inside** the number (none is bigger than the number, and 1 is always
the smallest), while **multiples grow outward** from the number (the number itself
is the smallest, and they go on forever). The number is the only thing that is
*both* a factor and a multiple of itself.

**Worked example:** For the number 6:
1. Factors of 6: 1, 2, 3, 6 → the least is **1**, the greatest is **6**, and
   there are only four (finite).
2. Multiples of 6: 6, 12, 18, 24 … → the least is **6**, and there is no greatest
   (infinite).
3. In a two-circle diagram, 6 sits in the overlap — it is a factor of 6 (6 ÷ 6 = 1)
   and a multiple of 6 (6 × 1 = 6).

**Try it yourself:** For the number 10, write its least factor, greatest factor and
least multiple. Then explain to a partner why you cannot write its greatest multiple.

## Trick 5: Count the Factors to Tell Prime from Composite

**Why it works:** A **prime** number has exactly two factors — 1 and itself. A
**composite** number has more than two. So the moment you find a third factor, you
know the number is composite. And remember the special case: **1 has only one
factor**, so it is *neither* prime nor composite. Also, 2 is the only even prime —
every other even number has 2 as an extra factor.

**Worked example:** Is 91 prime or composite?
1. 91 is odd, so 2 is not a factor.
2. 9 + 1 = 10, not divisible by 3, so 3 is not a factor.
3. It does not end in 0 or 5, so 5 is not a factor.
4. Try 7: 7 × 13 = 91 — a third factor! Factors are 1, 7, 13, 91.
5. 91 is **composite**, even though it *looks* prime.

**Try it yourself:** Decide whether 49, 53 and 57 are prime or composite by
hunting for a third factor. (Hint: try 3 and 7.)

## Trick 6: Factor Trees — Any Start, Same Prime Ending

**Why it works:** Prime factorisation means writing a number as a product of
*only* prime numbers. A factor tree splits the number into any factor pair, then
keeps splitting every composite branch until only primes are left. The amazing
part: whichever pair you start with, you always end with the **same set of
primes** — every composite number has a unique prime factorisation.

**Worked example:** Prime factorise 30 two different ways.
1. Start with 30 = 6 × 5. 5 is prime; split 6 = 2 × 3. Leaves: 2, 3, 5.
2. Start with 30 = 10 × 3. 3 is prime; split 10 = 2 × 5. Leaves: 2, 5, 3.
3. Both trees give **30 = 2 × 3 × 5**. Check: 2 × 3 = 6, 6 × 5 = 30 ✓.
4. Careful: "2 × 15" or "6 × 5" are NOT prime factorisations because 15 and 6
   are composite — keep splitting!

**Try it yourself:** Draw a factor tree for 36 starting with 4 × 9, then another
starting with 6 × 6. Do you get the same primes?

## Trick 7: The Last-Digit Rules for 10, 5 and 2

**Why it works:** Look at the times tables. Multiples of 10 all end in 0. Multiples
of 5 all end in 0 or 5. Multiples of 2 all end in 0, 2, 4, 6 or 8. So for these
three numbers you only need to look at the **ones digit** — no dividing needed!
Bonus: because every number ending in 0 also ends in "0 or 5" and in an even digit,
a number divisible by 10 is *always* divisible by 5 and by 2.

**Worked example:** Is 75,690 divisible by 2, 5 and 10?
1. The ones digit is 0.
2. 0 is even → divisible by 2 ✓.
3. 0 is "0 or 5" → divisible by 5 ✓.
4. 0 → divisible by 10 ✓.
5. Now try 1,245: it ends in 5, so it is divisible by 5 but **not** by 2 or 10.

**Try it yourself:** Sort these numbers into "divisible by 5 only" and "divisible
by both 5 and 10": 4,820; 1,245; 45,630; 48,965.

## Trick 8: The Last-TWO-Digits Rule for 4

**Why it works:** The ones digit alone cannot tell you about 4 — 12 and 22 both end
in 2, but only 12 is divisible by 4. The secret is that **100 = 25 × 4**, so every
hundred is already divisible by 4. That means you can ignore all the hundreds,
thousands and beyond, and only check the number made by the last two digits.

**Worked example:** Is 26,228 divisible by 4? What about 10,890?
1. For 26,228, the last two digits make 28. 28 ÷ 4 = 7 exactly → **yes**.
2. For 10,890, the last two digits make 90. 90 ÷ 4 = 22 remainder 2 → **no**
   (even though it ends in 0 and is divisible by 2, 5 and 10).
3. Remember: divisible by 4 always means divisible by 2, but divisible by 2 does
   **not** always mean divisible by 4.

**Try it yourself:** Use the last-two-digits rule on 2,024 and 1,903. Then explain
why 5,640 passes but 75,690 fails.

## Trick 9: The Digit-Sum Rule for 3

**Why it works:** Multiples of 3 can end in any digit (3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18 … end in
3, 6, 9, 2, 5, 8 …), so the last digit is useless. But look at the sums of the
digits: 12 → 1 + 2 = 3, 15 → 1 + 5 = 6, 18 → 1 + 8 = 9. The digit sum of a multiple
of 3 is always itself a multiple of 3. So add the digits — if the sum is in the
3 times table, the whole number is divisible by 3.

**Worked example:** Is 5,640 divisible by 3? Is 70,216?
1. 5 + 6 + 4 + 0 = 15. 15 = 3 × 5 → 5,640 **is** divisible by 3.
2. 7 + 0 + 2 + 1 + 6 = 16. 16 is not a multiple of 3 → 70,216 is **not**.
3. Combine rules! 24,825 ends in 5 (divisible by 5) and 2 + 4 + 8 + 2 + 5 = 21
   (divisible by 3), so it is divisible by both 3 and 5.

**Try it yourself:** Which numbers between 100 and 150 end in 0 or 5 AND have a
digit sum divisible by 3? (You should find three.)

## Trick 10: Solve "Which Number Fits Both Clues?" Puzzles by Listing

**Why it works:** Puzzles like "a multiple of 5 that is more than 70 and 1 more
than a multiple of 9" look scary, but you never need to guess. List the numbers
that satisfy the *easy* clue first (the multiples), then test each one against the
second clue. The first number that passes both is your answer.

**Worked example:** Find the multiple of 5 that is more than 70 and is exactly
1 more than a multiple of 9.
1. Multiples of 5 above 70: 75, 80, 85, 90, 95, 100 …
2. "1 more than a multiple of 9" → subtract 1 and check: 74, 79, 84, 89, 94, 99.
3. Is any of these in the 9 times table? 9 × 11 = 99 ✓ (the others are not).
4. 99 came from 100, so the answer is **100**.

**Try it yourself:** Find the multiple of 9 that is greater than 60 and exactly
4 less than a multiple of 5. (List multiples of 9 first, then add 4 to each.)
