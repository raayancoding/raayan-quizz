# Perimeter & Area — Tips and Tricks for Smart Kids! 🧮📏

Hi Grade 4 explorer! Perimeter and area sound like big words, but once you know
the tricks below, you'll be solving them in a flash. Read each trick, follow
the worked example with your pencil, and then try the mini practice yourself!

---

## Trick 1: The Rectangle Perimeter Shortcut — P = 2 × (l + b)

**Why it works:** A rectangle always has TWO pairs of equal sides — the top
and bottom are the same length, and the two sides (left and right) are the
same breadth. So instead of writing out `length + breadth + length + breadth`
and adding four numbers one by one, you can just add the length and breadth
ONCE, then double the answer. That's exactly the same total, just faster!

**Worked example:** A photo frame is 20 cm long and 16 cm wide (just like the
one in the book). Instead of adding 20 + 16 + 20 + 16:
1. Add length + breadth: 20 + 16 = 36 cm
2. Double it: 2 × 36 = 72 cm

So the perimeter is 72 cm — the exact same answer as adding all four sides,
but in two quick steps instead of four!

**Try it yourself:** A rectangular garden is 15 m long and 9 m wide. Use the
shortcut to find how much fencing wire is needed to go around it.

---

## Trick 2: The Square Perimeter Shortcut — P = 4 × side

**Why it works:** A square is extra special — ALL 4 sides are exactly the
same length. So there's no need to add the same number four times; you can
simply multiply it by 4.

**Worked example:** A square farm has a side of 10 m (like the fencing
problem in the book).
1. Instead of 10 + 10 + 10 + 10...
2. Just multiply: 4 × 10 = 40 m

So the farmer needs 40 m of fencing.

**Try it yourself:** A square handkerchief has a side of 22 cm. How much
ribbon is needed to trim all the way around its edge?

---

## Trick 3: Area of a Rectangle — A = l × b (Why It's Really Just Counting Squares)

**Why it works:** Area means "how many unit squares cover the shape." If you
imagine a rectangle as being built from rows and columns of little 1×1
squares — like an egg carton or a chocolate bar — then the total number of
squares is simply (number of rows) × (number of squares in each row). That's
the same as multiplying length × breadth!

**Worked example:** A rectangle is 5 cm long and 4 cm wide (just like the
book's chocolate-bar picture).
1. Imagine 4 rows of squares, with 5 squares in each row.
2. Total squares = 4 × 5 = 20
3. So the area is 20 sq cm.

**Try it yourself:** A table top is 6 units long and 3 units wide. How many
unit squares would it take to cover it completely?

---

## Trick 4: Area of a Square — A = side × side (NOT 4 × side!)

**Why it works:** Since all 4 sides of a square are equal, the area formula
is just "side × side" — imagine rows and columns that are both the same
length. Be careful: `4 × side` gives you the **perimeter**, not the area!
Mixing these two up is the #1 mistake kids make, so always ask yourself:
"Am I finding the boundary (×4) or the space inside (×itself)?"

**Worked example:** A square has a side of 3 cm.
- Perimeter = 4 × 3 = **12 cm** (a length, going around)
- Area = 3 × 3 = **9 sq cm** (a covered space, filling inside)

Notice the units are different too — cm for perimeter, but sq cm (cm²) for
area. That's your clue to check which one you calculated!

**Try it yourself:** A square sticker has a side of 6 cm. Find BOTH its
perimeter and its area, and label each answer with the correct unit.

## Trick 5: Telling Perimeter and Area Apart — "Walking Around" vs "Covering Inside"

**Why it works:** The easiest way to never mix these up is to picture the
action in the question:
- If you're **walking, jogging, fencing, or decorating an edge** → that's
  the **boundary** → you need **PERIMETER**.
- If you're **covering, tiling, painting, or planting** a flat surface →
  that's the **space inside** → you need **AREA**.

**Worked example:** From the book:
- "Ravi wants to cover one of his kitchen walls with tiles." → He is
  COVERING a surface, so he needs the wall's **AREA**.
- "Maya jogs around a rectangular ground." → She is WALKING the BOUNDARY,
  so that distance is the ground's **PERIMETER**.

**Try it yourself:** Read this sentence: "Priya wants to paint the floor of
her room." Is this an area question or a perimeter question? Explain why in
one sentence.

## Trick 6: Finding a Missing Side When You Know the Perimeter

**Why it works:** If you already know the total perimeter AND all the other
side lengths, you can find the missing one by simple subtraction: just
subtract the sum of the known sides from the total perimeter. Whatever is
left over must be the missing side!

**Worked example:** A triangle has two sides of 5 cm each, and its perimeter
is 14 cm. Find the third side.
1. Add the known sides: 5 + 5 = 10 cm
2. Subtract from the total perimeter: 14 − 10 = 4 cm
3. So the missing side is 4 cm.

Always double-check: 5 + 5 + 4 = 14 cm ✓ — it matches the perimeter given!

**Try it yourself:** A quadrilateral has a perimeter of 26 cm. Three of its
sides are 6 cm, 8 cm and 5 cm. Find the fourth side.

## Trick 7: The Corner-Cut Surprise — Cutting a Piece Out Doesn't Always Shrink the Perimeter!

**Why it works:** This is a favourite "gotcha" in the book! When you cut a
small square notch out of a bigger square's corner, you might think the
perimeter gets smaller because you removed material — but it doesn't! You
lose two short edges where you cut, but you gain two brand-new edges along
the step of the notch, and these cancel out exactly.

**Worked example:** An 8 cm square has a perimeter of 4 × 8 = 32 cm. Now cut
out a 3 cm × 3 cm square from one corner:
1. You remove 3 cm from TWO of the original sides (that's −3 −3).
2. But the cut creates TWO brand new edges of 3 cm each (that's +3 +3).
3. Net change: −3 −3 +3 +3 = 0 → the perimeter is STILL 32 cm!

Meanwhile, the AREA does shrink: 8×8 − 3×3 = 64 − 9 = 55 sq cm. So perimeter
and area don't always change together — that's the surprise!

**Try it yourself:** Draw a 6 cm × 6 cm square on paper, then cut out a
2 cm × 2 cm notch from one corner. Predict the new perimeter BEFORE you
calculate it, then check your answer using the trick above.

## Trick 8: Same Area Can Have Different Perimeters (and Vice Versa!) — The "Compact Shape" Rule

**Why it works:** Rectangles with the exact same area can look very
different — one long and skinny, one short and squarish (compact). The book
discovered a neat pattern: for the SAME area, the more compact (square-like)
rectangle always has the SMALLER perimeter. And for the SAME perimeter, the
more compact rectangle always encloses the GREATER area.

**Worked example:** Compare two rectangles, both with area = 12 sq units:
- 1 × 12 rectangle (long and thin): Perimeter = 2×(1+12) = 26 units
- 3 × 4 rectangle (more compact): Perimeter = 2×(3+4) = 14 units

Same area (12 sq units), but very different perimeters! The compact shape
"wastes" less boundary.

**Try it yourself:** List all the whole-number rectangles with an area of
16 sq units (hint: think of pairs of numbers that multiply to 16). Calculate
each one's perimeter. Which shape is the most compact, and does it have the
smallest perimeter?

## Trick 9: Always Write the Correct Unit — cm vs cm² (and Why It Matters)

**Why it works:** Perimeter is a LENGTH, so it's measured in plain units:
cm, m, km. Area is a SURFACE, so it's measured in SQUARE units: cm², m²,
km² (or "sq cm", "sq m", "sq km"). Writing "20 cm" when you mean area, or
"20 cm²" when you mean perimeter, is a mistake that can cost you marks even
if your number is correct — so always attach the right unit as your very
last step!

**Worked example:** A rectangle is 5 cm by 4 cm.
- Perimeter = 2×(5+4) = 18 **cm** (a boundary length)
- Area = 5×4 = 20 **sq cm** (a covered surface)

Same rectangle, same numbers 5 and 4 — but completely different units
because they measure completely different things!

**Try it yourself:** Look back at three answers you found while practising
perimeter or area problems. Check each one: did you write cm or sq cm? Fix
any that used the wrong unit.
