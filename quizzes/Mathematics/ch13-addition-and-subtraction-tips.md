## 1. Line Up the Ones First (the place-value table trick)

**Why it works:** Every digit has a job — ones, tens, hundreds, thousands, ten-thousands, lakhs. Addition and subtraction only work when you add *ones to ones* and *tens to tens*. If the numbers have different lengths (like 6,07,189 and 2,500), lining them up from the LEFT gives nonsense. Lining them up from the RIGHT keeps each digit in its own column.

**Worked example:** 6,07,189 + 50,310 + 2,500

```
  L  TTh  Th  H  T  O
  6   0    7  1  8  9
      5    0  3  1  0
           2  5  0  0
  --------------------
  6   5    9  9  9  9   → 6,59,999
```

Start at the ones: 9 + 0 + 0 = 9. Then tens 8 + 1 + 0 = 9, hundreds 1 + 3 + 5 = 9, thousands 7 + 0 + 2 = 9, ten-thousands 0 + 5 = 5, lakhs 6.

**Try it yourself:** Arrange and add 36,484 + 26,525 + 1,28,303. Which number sits furthest to the left?

## 2. Carry Like a Relay Runner (regrouping in addition)

**Why it works:** A single column can only hold one digit (0–9). The moment a column total reaches 10 or more, the "10" part is really one unit of the NEXT place: 10 ones = 1 ten, 10 tens = 1 hundred, 10 hundreds = 1 thousand. So you write the leftover digit and pass the 1 along — like a baton.

**Worked example:** 64,875 + 34,546

1. Ones: 5 + 6 = 11 → write **1**, carry 1 to the tens.
2. Tens: 1 + 7 + 4 = 12 → write **2**, carry 1 to the hundreds.
3. Hundreds: 1 + 8 + 5 = 14 → write **4**, carry 1 to the thousands.
4. Thousands: 1 + 4 + 4 = 9 → write **9** (no carry).
5. Ten-thousands: 6 + 3 = 9 → write **9**.

Answer: **99,421**.

**Try it yourself:** Add 55,555 + 66,666. How many times do you carry?

## 3. Expanded Form — Add the "Big Pieces" Separately

**Why it works:** 52,765 is really 50,000 + 2,000 + 700 + 60 + 5. If you add each piece to its matching piece in the other number, you get the same total as the column method — but you can SEE every step, which makes it a perfect way to check your work.

**Worked example:** 52,765 + 36,134

```
50,000 + 2,000 + 700 + 60 + 5
30,000 + 6,000 + 100 + 30 + 4
------------------------------
80,000 + 8,000 + 800 + 90 + 9 = 88,899
```

(If a piece goes over, regroup it: 1,300 + 110 + 11 becomes 1,421.)

**Try it yourself:** Write 24,563 and 13,479 in expanded form and add the pieces. Then add them in columns — do you get the same answer?

## 4. Borrow Through the Zeros (regrouping in subtraction)

**Why it works:** When the top digit is smaller than the bottom one, you borrow 1 from the next place to the left — it becomes 10 in your column. If that next place is a **0**, it has nothing to lend, so you go one more step left, borrow there, and the 0 becomes 9 (10 minus the 1 it passed on).

**Worked example:** 5,24,700 − 3,16,672

- Ones: 0 − 2? Can't. Tens is 0 too, so borrow from the hundreds: hundreds 7 → **6**, tens 0 → **9**, ones 0 → **10**.
- Ones: 10 − 2 = 8. Tens: 9 − 7 = 2. Hundreds: 6 − 6 = 0.
- Thousands: 4 − 6? Borrow: ten-thousands 2 → 1, thousands → 14. 14 − 6 = 8.
- Ten-thousands: 1 − 1 = 0. Lakhs: 5 − 3 = 2.

Answer: **2,08,028**.

**Try it yourself:** Subtract 1,20,000 from 4,00,000 — how many zeros do you have to borrow through?

## 5. The Add-Back Check (addition and subtraction are reverse operations)

**Why it works:** Subtraction takes a part away from a whole. Adding that part back must rebuild the whole: **difference + subtrahend = minuend**. If it doesn't, you made a slip somewhere. This is why every addition fact gives you TWO subtraction facts for free.

**Worked example:** 65,432 − 26,524 = 38,908

Check: 38,908 + 26,524

```
  38,908
+ 26,524
--------
  65,432  ✓ (the minuend!)
```

And from 34,867 + 15,120 = 49,987 you instantly know: 49,987 − 34,867 = 15,120 and 49,987 − 15,120 = 34,867.

**Try it yourself:** Find 52,588 − 17,809, then prove your answer with one addition.

## 6. Ryan's Round-Number Trick (make a 1,00,000 first)

**Why it works:** Moving 1 from one addend to the other doesn't change the total — you only shifted it across. So if one number is just below a round number (like 99,999), steal 1 from its partner, make it round, and add in your head.

**Worked example:** 99,999 + 2,423

- Take 1 from 2,423 → 2,422. Give it to 99,999 → 1,00,000.
- 1,00,000 + 2,422 = **1,02,422**. No carrying at all!

**Try it yourself:** Solve 19,999 + 1,295 Ryan's way — without any column addition.

## 7. Mrs. Mehta's Slide-Down Trick (subtracting from 10,000)

**Why it works:** If you take the SAME amount away from both the minuend and the subtrahend, the gap between them doesn't change. Slide both numbers down by 1 and a scary 10,000 becomes a friendly 9,999 — a number that never needs borrowing.

**Worked example:** 10,000 − 111

- Slide both down by 1: 9,999 − 110.
- 9 − 0 = 9, 9 − 1 = 8, 9 − 1 = 8, 9 − 0 = 9 → **9,889**.

So 10,000 − 111 = 9,889 too.

**Try it yourself:** Use the slide-down trick to work out 1,000 − 117 in your head.

## 8. Round, Then Estimate (quick answers when "about" is enough)

**Why it works:** Round numbers are easy to add and subtract mentally. The book's rule: a 4-digit number → round to the nearest **hundred**; a 3-digit number → round to the nearest **ten**. Rounding to a *lower* place gives a *closer* estimate, but for huge numbers it stops being quick.

**Worked example:** Mr. Bajaj wants an AC for ₹8,340 and a fridge for ₹2,970. Is ₹12,000 enough?

- ₹8,340 → ₹8,300 (tens digit 4, round down). ₹2,970 → ₹3,000 (tens digit 7, round up).
- ₹8,300 + ₹3,000 = **₹11,300**, which is under ₹12,000 — yes, it's enough.

(The exact total is ₹11,310 — only ₹10 away.)

**Try it yourself:** A plantation had 5,689 trees and 1,853 were damaged. Estimate how many are left, once to the nearest ten and once to the nearest hundred. Which estimate do you think is closer?

## 9. Walk Backwards (the backward strategy)

**Why it works:** Some problems tell you the END and ask for the START. Reverse the story: undo the LAST event first, and swap every operation (if they gave away, you add back; if they received, you subtract). Step by step you arrive at the beginning.

**Worked example:** Amy got 18 marbles from a friend, gave 10 to her brother and 8 to her cousin, and has 40 left. How many did she start with?

1. Last event: she gave away 10 + 8 = 18. Undo it: 40 + 18 = 58.
2. Before that: she received 18. Undo it: 58 − 18 = 40.

Amy started with **40 marbles**.

**Try it yourself:** Ravi got 20 coins from Priya, gave 15 each to Ria and Arjun, and now has 60. Walk backwards to find how many he had at first.

## 10. Break it, Spot it, Filter it, Solve it (computational thinking)

**Why it works:** Long word problems hide a short maths problem inside them. Four steps dig it out: **Break it** (write the numbers as short statements), **Spot it** (is it a total → add, or a comparison / taking away → subtract?), **Filter it** (ignore the story words), **Solve it** (apply the rules, regrouping where needed). Two-step problems just repeat "Spot it" twice.

**Worked example:** Ritika bought a notebook for ₹25 and a pen for ₹135, and got a ₹20 discount. How much did she pay?

- Break it: notebook 25, pen 135, discount 20.
- Spot it: total cost → **add**; discount → **subtract**. (Two steps, two different operations.)
- Filter it: only the three money amounts matter.
- Solve it: 25 + 135 = 160, then 160 − 20 = **₹140**.

**Try it yourself:** A shopkeeper had 60,000 uniforms, sold 12,372 in week 1 and 23,003 in week 2. Write the four steps, then find how many are left.
