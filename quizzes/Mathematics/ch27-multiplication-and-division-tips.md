# Multiplication and Division — Tips and Tricks for Smart Kids! ✖️➗

Hi Grade 4 mathematician! Multiplying and dividing big numbers can feel slow
if you always do it the long way. But once you know the tricks below, you'll
be able to solve many problems in your head — or much faster on paper! Read
each trick, follow the worked example with your pencil, and then try the mini
practice yourself.

---

## Trick 1: Multiplying by 10, 100, or 1000 — Just Shift the Digits!

**Why it works:** Our number system is built on place value (ones, tens,
hundreds...). When you multiply by 10, every digit moves one place to the
LEFT — which looks like "attaching a zero." Multiply by 100 and digits shift
two places (attach two zeros); by 1,000, three places (attach three zeros).

**Worked example:** What is 408 × 1,000?
1. Multiplying by 1,000 shifts every digit three places left.
2. That's the same as attaching three zeros to 408.
3. 408 × 1,000 = 4,08,000.

**Try it yourself:** What is 72 × 100? What is 35 × 10?

---

## Trick 2: Multiplying by 5 — Multiply by 10, Then Halve It

**Why it works:** 5 is exactly half of 10. So instead of multiplying by 5
directly, you can multiply by 10 (super easy — just attach a zero) and then
cut your answer in half.

**Worked example:** What is 235 × 5?
1. First multiply by 10: 235 × 10 = 2,350.
2. Now halve it: 2,350 ÷ 2 = 1,175.
3. So 235 × 5 = 1,175.

**Try it yourself:** Use the ×10-then-halve trick to find 46 × 5.

---

## Trick 3: Multiplying by 9 — Multiply by 10, Then Subtract Once

**Why it works:** 9 is just 1 less than 10. So 9 × any number is the same as
10 × that number, minus that number once. This "smart method" is exactly
what the book uses for numbers close to round numbers like 99, 98, or 101!

**Worked example:** What is 23 × 99 (that is, 23 × (100 − 1))?
1. Multiply by the round number first: 23 × 100 = 2,300.
2. Since 99 is 1 less than 100, subtract 23 once: 2,300 − 23 = 2,277.
3. So 23 × 99 = 2,277.

This same idea works for ×9, ×999, or even numbers like ×101 (multiply by
100, then ADD once instead of subtracting) — always ask "how far is this
number from a nice round number, and in which direction?"

**Try it yourself:** Use the smart method to find 6 × 9 (hint: 6 × 10, then
subtract 6 once).

## Trick 4: The Doubling-and-Halving Trick

**Why it works:** If you double one number and halve the other, the product
stays exactly the same! This lets you turn an awkward multiplication into an
easier one — especially useful when one of the numbers is even.

**Worked example:** What is 18 × 6?
1. Split 18 into 2 × 9.
2. Multiply the easier pair first: 9 × 6 = 54.
3. Double it (to account for the "2" you took out): 2 × 54 = 108.
4. So 18 × 6 = 108.

For bigger numbers, you can keep halving-and-doubling in a table until one
side becomes 1 — that's exactly how the book solves 8 × 45 by turning it
into (4, 90) → (2, 180) → (1, 360), so 8 × 45 = 360!

**Try it yourself:** Use doubling-and-halving to find 24 × 8.

## Trick 5: Split the Number (Distributive Trick) for 2-Digit Multiplication

**Why it works:** Any number can be broken into friendlier pieces — like
tens and ones — multiplied separately, and then added back together. This is
called the distributive property, and it's the secret behind almost every
multiplication trick in this chapter!

**Worked example:** What is 41 × 19?
1. Split 41 into 40 + 1.
2. Multiply each part by 19: 40 × 19 = 760, and 1 × 19 = 19.
3. Add the two pieces: 760 + 19 = 779.
4. So 41 × 19 = 779.

**Try it yourself:** Split 52 into 50 + 2 to find 52 × 23.

---

## Trick 6: The Column Method, Step by Step

**Why it works:** For big multiplications, the column method organizes the
distributive trick neatly on paper — multiply by the ones digit first, then
the tens digit (shifted one place left), then the hundreds digit (shifted
two places left), and finally add everything up.

**Worked example:** What is 2,906 × 32?
1. Multiply by the ones digit (2): 2,906 × 2 = 5,812.
2. Multiply by the tens digit (3, worth 30), shifted one place left:
   2,906 × 3 = 8,718 → written as 87,180.
3. Add the two partial products: 5,812 + 87,180 = 92,992.
4. So 2,906 × 32 = 92,992.

**Try it yourself:** Use the column method to find 1,257 × 6.

## Trick 7: Check Your Multiplication with Inverse Division

**Why it works:** Multiplication and division are opposite (inverse)
operations — they "undo" each other. If 6 × 7 = 42, then 42 ÷ 6 must equal 7,
and 42 ÷ 7 must equal 6. You can use this to double-check your work!

**Worked example:** You calculated 517 × 815 = 4,21,355. How can you check
part of it?
1. Since 517 = 500 + 17, check the piece: 4,21,355 − 4,07,500 (which is
   500 × 815) should equal 17 × 815.
2. 4,21,355 − 4,07,500 = 13,855.
3. Now verify with multiplication: 17 × 815 = 815 × 10 + 815 × 7 =
   8,150 + 5,705 = 13,855. ✓ It matches!

**Try it yourself:** If 12 × 4 = 48, write down the TWO division facts that
must also be true.

## Trick 8: Long Division — Divide, Multiply, Subtract, Bring Down

**Why it works:** Long division always repeats the SAME four steps in the
SAME order. Once you memorize this rhythm, you can divide any big number,
one digit at a time.

**Worked example:** What is 3,575 ÷ 5?
1. **Divide:** How many times does 5 go into the first digit(s)? 5 into 3
   doesn't work, so use 35: 35 ÷ 5 = 7.
2. **Multiply:** 7 × 5 = 35.
3. **Subtract:** 35 − 35 = 0.
4. **Bring down** the next digit (7), making 07, and repeat: 7 ÷ 5 = 1
   remainder 2. Bring down the next digit (5) to make 25: 25 ÷ 5 = 5.
5. Putting the quotient digits together: 3,575 ÷ 5 = 715, remainder 0.

Whenever you finish, you can verify using: Quotient × Divisor + Remainder =
Dividend. Check: 715 × 5 + 0 = 3,575. ✓

**Try it yourself:** Use the 4-step process (Divide → Multiply → Subtract →
Bring down) to work out 768 ÷ 4.

## Trick 9: Quick Divisibility Rules — Spot Factors Instantly

**Why it works:** You don't always need to do the whole division to know
whether a number divides evenly. A few quick checks on the digits can tell
you right away:
- **Divisible by 2:** the last digit is even (0, 2, 4, 6, or 8).
- **Divisible by 5:** the last digit is 0 or 5.
- **Divisible by 10:** the last digit is 0.
- **Divisible by 3:** add up all the digits — if THAT sum is divisible by 3,
  so is the original number!

**Worked example:** Is 732 divisible by 3?
1. Add the digits: 7 + 3 + 2 = 12.
2. Is 12 divisible by 3? Yes (12 ÷ 3 = 4).
3. So 732 IS divisible by 3 — no long division needed to find out!

**Try it yourself:** Without dividing, figure out whether 108 is divisible
by 2, by 5, and by 3.

## Trick 10: Estimate First — Is Your Answer in the Right Ballpark?

**Why it works:** Before (or after) doing an exact calculation, rounding
each number to a nearby "friendly" number (like the nearest 10 or 100) lets
you quickly predict roughly what the answer should be. If your exact answer
is wildly different from your estimate, you probably made a mistake
somewhere!

**Worked example:** Estimate 918 × 26.
1. Round 918 to the nearest 10: 920.
2. Round 26 to the nearest 10: 30.
3. Multiply the rounded numbers: 920 × 30 = 27,600.
4. So the exact answer should be somewhere close to 27,600 — a quick way to
   sanity-check your work.

**Try it yourself:** Estimate 4,956 ÷ 17 by rounding 4,956 to the nearest 100
and 17 to the nearest 10, then dividing the rounded numbers.

feel like a fun puzzle instead of hard work. Good luck, math explorer! 🎉
