# Formation of Numbers — Tips and Tricks for Smart Kids! 🔢✨

Hi Grade 4 number explorer! Big numbers like 27,20,159 or 5,716,146 can look
scary, but they are just small numbers wearing a long coat. Each trick below
comes straight from your Formation of Numbers chapter. Read the trick, follow
the worked example with your pencil, then try the practice yourself!

---

## Trick 1: Chop the Number into Periods Before You Read It

**Why it works:** In the Indian Number System, the digits are grouped into
*periods*: the ONES period (3 places: hundreds, tens, ones), then the
THOUSANDS period (2 places) and then the LAKHS period (2 places). Commas mark
the periods. If you read one period at a time and say its name, a 7-digit
number becomes three tiny numbers. And remember the book's rule: we never say
the plural ("thousand", not "thousands") and we never say the word "ones".

**Worked example:** Read 18,36,591.
1. Put the periods in from the right: 3 digits, then 2, then 2 → 18 | 36 | 591.
2. Say each period with its name: "eighteen **lakh**", "thirty-six
   **thousand**", "five hundred ninety-one".
3. Join them: *Eighteen lakh thirty-six thousand five hundred ninety-one*.

**Try it yourself:** Chop 32,71,025 into periods and write its number name.

## Trick 2: The Comma Rule — 3, then 2, 2 (Indian) or 3, 3, 3 (International)

**Why it works:** Both systems start with a comma after the first three digits
from the right. After that, the Indian system adds a comma every **two**
digits (lakhs, crores), while the International system adds a comma every
**three** digits (thousands, millions). The digits never change — only the
commas and the names do. That is why 10,00,000 (ten lakh) and 1,000,000 (one
million) are exactly the same amount.

**Worked example:** Write 741025 in both systems.
1. Count from the right. Indian: 741025 → 7,41,025 → *seven lakh forty-one
   thousand twenty-five*.
2. International: 741025 → 741,025 → *seven hundred forty-one thousand
   twenty-five*.
3. Same digits, same value, different commas and names!

**Try it yourself:** Write 2003120 with commas in the Indian system and in the
International system, and write both number names.

## Trick 3: Place Value vs Face Value — Ask "Where is it sitting?"

**Why it works:** The *face value* of a digit is just the digit itself. The
*place value* is the digit multiplied by the value of its seat in the chart
(ones, tens, hundreds, thousands...). So to find a place value, first find the
digit's seat, then multiply. The only digits whose place value equals their
face value are the digit in the **ones** place and any **zero** (because
0 × anything = 0).

**Worked example:** Find the place value and face value of 6 in 5,67,849.
1. Label the seats from the right: 9 ones, 4 tens, 8 hundreds, 7 thousands,
   **6 ten thousands**, 5 lakhs.
2. Face value of 6 = 6.
3. Place value of 6 = 6 × 10,000 = **60,000** (not 6,00,000 — that would be
   the lakhs seat!).
4. Bonus: difference = 60,000 − 6 = 59,994.

**Try it yourself:** Find the place value, face value and their difference for
the digit 7 in 576,403.

## Trick 4: Expanded Form — Zero Is a Placeholder, Not a Term

**Why it works:** Expanded form is simply the place values of all the digits
added together. A zero digit has a place value of 0, so it adds nothing — you
just skip it. But when you go the other way (expanded → standard form), every
seat that has no term must get a **0** written in it, or the number shrinks!

**Worked example:** Write the standard form of 6,00,000 + 50,000 + 800 + 7.
1. Draw the seats: L | TTh | Th | H | T | O.
2. Fill what you have: 6 in lakhs, 5 in ten thousands, 8 in hundreds, 7 in ones.
3. Empty seats (thousands, tens) get a 0: 6 | 5 | **0** | 8 | **0** | 7.
4. Standard form: **6,50,807**. (Forgetting the zeros would give 6,587 — a
   totally different number.)

**Try it yourself:** Write 10,00,000 + 7,00,000 + 6,000 + 7 in standard form.

## Trick 5: "How Many Tens / Hundreds / Thousands?" — Just Cover the Digits

**Why it works:** Every place in the chart is 10 times the place to its right.
So to find how many *tens* are in a number, cover the ones digit and read what
is left; for *hundreds*, cover the last two digits; for *thousands*, cover the
last three. The uncovered part is your answer and the covered part is the
leftover.

**Worked example:** How many tens are there in 71,005?
1. Cover the ones digit (5): you can see 7100.
2. So there are **7,100 tens** and 5 ones left over.
3. Check: 7,100 × 10 = 71,000, and 71,000 + 5 = 71,005. ✔

**Try it yourself:** How many hundreds are there in 670,912? How many
thousands?

## Trick 6: Compare Numbers — Count Digits First, Then Walk from the Left

**Why it works:** A number with more digits is always bigger, so counting
digits settles most comparisons instantly. If the digit counts are equal, line
the numbers up in a place value chart and compare from the **leftmost** digit;
the first place where the digits differ decides the winner. If one number is
written in the Indian system and the other in the International system, don't
panic — line up the digits and compare exactly the same way.

**Worked example:** Which is smaller, 76,52,103 or 76,97,321?
1. Both have 7 digits, so go to step 2.
2. Ten lakhs: 7 = 7. Lakhs: 6 = 6. Ten thousands: 5 vs 9 — different!
3. 5 < 9, so **76,52,103 < 76,97,321**.

**Try it yourself:** Use the same steps to arrange 6,32,894; 6,83,294;
6,23,849; 6,82,394 in ascending order.

## Trick 7: Greatest and Smallest Numbers — Sort the Digits, Watch the Zero

**Why it works:** The leftmost seat has the biggest place value. To make the
**greatest** number, put the digits in descending order so the biggest digit
gets the biggest seat. To make the **smallest** number, put them in ascending
order — but a zero can never go first (02,689 is really 2,689, a 4-digit
number!), so zero slides into the **second** seat from the left.

**Worked example:** Use 9, 8, 0, 1, 4, 5, 6 once each to make the greatest
and smallest 7-digit numbers.
1. Greatest: descending order 9, 8, 6, 5, 4, 1, 0 → **98,65,410**.
2. Smallest: ascending order 0, 1, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9. Zero can't lead, so swap it
   with the 1 → 1, 0, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9 → **10,45,689**.

**Try it yourself:** Using the digits 6, 9, 4, 2, 7, 5 once each, form the
greatest 6-digit number and the greatest number that has 7 in the tens place.

## Trick 8: Predecessor and Successor — Watch Out for the Roll-Over

**Why it works:** The successor is just the number + 1 and the predecessor is
the number − 1. Usually only the ones digit changes. But when a number ends in
9 (or several 9s), adding 1 makes those 9s roll over to 0 and carries 1 to the
next seat. Likewise, when it ends in 0, subtracting 1 needs a borrow, so more
than one digit changes.

**Worked example:** Find the successor of 41,39,999 and the predecessor of
7,50,110.
1. 41,39,999 + 1: the three 9s become 0 0 0 and carry 1 into the ten thousands
   seat: 3 becomes 4 → **41,40,000**.
2. 7,50,110 − 1: ones digit is 0, so borrow from the tens: 1 ten becomes 0 tens
   and 10 ones; 10 − 1 = 9 → **7,50,109**.

**Try it yourself:** A step counter shows 1,99,999. What will it show after one
more step? And what did it show one step earlier?

## Trick 9: Rounding — Look at ONE Digit to the Right, and 5 Goes Up

**Why it works:** To round to the nearest ten, look only at the ones digit; to
round to the nearest hundred, look only at the tens digit. If that digit is 0,
1, 2, 3 or 4, keep the same ten/hundred and write zeros after it. If it is 5,
6, 7, 8 or 9, go up to the next ten/hundred. The halfway number (5) always
rounds **up** — 125 → 130 and 350 → 400.

**Worked example:** Round 9,339 to the nearest ten and nearest hundred, and
decide which estimate is closer.
1. Nearest ten: ones digit is 9 → go up → **9,340**.
2. Nearest hundred: tens digit is 3 → stay → **9,300**.
3. Closer estimate: 9,340 is only 1 away, 9,300 is 39 away, so **9,340** is
   closer. (Rounding to the nearest ten is always at least as close as
   rounding to the nearest hundred.)

**Try it yourself:** Round 4,786 to the nearest ten and to the nearest hundred.
Then round 4,555 to the nearest hundred — remember what 5 does!

## Trick 10: Lakhs ↔ Millions Converter

**Why it works:** The two systems share the same digits, so you only need two
facts: **1 lakh = 100,000 (one hundred thousand)** and **10 lakh = 1,000,000
(one million)**. Any Indian number can be turned into an International one by
removing the Indian commas and regrouping in threes from the right.

**Worked example:** A cheque says ₹12,34,571. Write it in the International
system.
1. Remove the commas: 1234571.
2. Regroup in threes from the right: 1 | 234 | 571 → **1,234,571**.
3. Read it: *one million two hundred thirty-four thousand five hundred
   seventy-one*. (Check: 12 lakh ≈ 1.2 million ✔)

**Try it yourself:** The population of Lakshadweep is one lakh forty-three
thousand four hundred eighty-eight. Write it as a numeral in the International
Number System.

---

Keep these tricks handy, and remember: every giant number is just ones, tens,
hundreds and friends standing in a line. Happy counting! 🎉
