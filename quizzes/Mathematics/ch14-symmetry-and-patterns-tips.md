# Symmetry and Patterns — Tips & Tricks (Chapter 14, Grade 4)

A collection of simple tricks that help you spot symmetry, complete reflections,
tell if shapes will tessellate, and crack number patterns — with easy,
step-by-step examples you can follow.

---

## 1. The Fold-and-Check Trick for Finding Lines of Symmetry

**Why it works:** A line of symmetry is a line where, if you folded the shape
along it, both halves would land exactly on top of each other. Since you can't
always fold a drawing, imagining the fold (or actually tracing and folding
paper) is the fastest way to test any line without guessing.

**Step-by-step example — Does a heart shape have a line of symmetry?**
1. Look for a line down the middle of the heart, splitting it into a left half
   and a right half.
2. Imagine folding the heart along that line.
3. Check: does the left lobe land exactly on the right lobe? Does the point at
   the bottom line up with itself?
4. Yes — both halves match perfectly, so that vertical line **is** a line of
   symmetry. A heart has exactly 1 line of symmetry.
5. Now try folding it any other way (sideways, diagonally) — the halves won't
   match, so there is only that one line.

**Try it yourself:** Draw a plain arrow pointing straight up. Imagine folding
it down the middle. Does it have a line of symmetry? How many lines do you
think it has in total?

## 2. Know Your Shapes' Symmetry "Cheat Sheet"

**Why it works:** Regular shapes (where all sides and angles are equal) follow
a neat rule: **the number of lines of symmetry usually matches the number of
sides.** Knowing this lets you predict the answer instantly instead of folding
every time.

**Step-by-step example:**
1. A square has 4 equal sides → it has 4 lines of symmetry (2 through the
   middle of opposite sides, 2 through opposite corners).
2. An equilateral triangle has 3 equal sides → it has 3 lines of symmetry.
3. A regular pentagon has 5 equal sides → it has 5 lines of symmetry.
4. A regular hexagon has 6 equal sides → it has 6 lines of symmetry.
5. A circle has no straight sides at all — every line through its centre is a
   line of symmetry, so it has **infinite** lines of symmetry.
6. Watch out: this "sides = lines" rule only works for *regular* shapes. A
   plain rectangle (not a square) has 4 sides but only 2 lines of symmetry,
   because its sides are not all equal.

**Try it yourself:** A stop-sign shape (a regular octagon) has 8 equal sides.
Using the cheat sheet rule, how many lines of symmetry should it have?

## 3. The Mirror-Flip Trick for Reflection Puzzles

**Why it works:** Reflecting something in a mirror line flips its direction
but never changes its size or shape. So every point of the reflected image is
the *same distance* from the mirror line as the matching point on the
original — just on the opposite side.

**Step-by-step example — Completing a mirror picture:**
1. Look at the half-picture and find the mirror (dotted) line.
2. Pick one point on the drawn half — say, the tip of a shape 3 dot-spaces
   away from the mirror line.
3. On the *other* side of the mirror line, mark a point exactly 3 dot-spaces
   away too (same height, opposite side).
4. Repeat for every important point (corners, curves, tips).
5. Join the new points up the same way as the original — you now have a
   perfect mirror image!

**Try it yourself:** If a triangle's corner is 4 dots to the left of a
vertical mirror line, how many dots to the *right* of the line should its
reflected corner be?

## 4. Spotting Whether Shapes Will Tessellate

**Why it works:** A tessellation must cover a surface with **no gaps and no
overlaps**. Shapes with straight edges that fit snugly against each other
(like squares, rectangles, and triangles) can do this. Shapes with curved
edges (like circles) always leave curved gaps no matter how you arrange them.

**Step-by-step example — Which of these tessellate: square, circle, triangle,
rectangle?**
1. Try lining up squares edge to edge — their straight sides match up
   perfectly with no gaps. ✅ Squares tessellate.
2. Try lining up triangles — two triangles can be joined to form a
   parallelogram or hexagon shape with no gaps. ✅ Triangles tessellate.
3. Try lining up rectangles — like bricks in a wall, they stack with no gaps.
   ✅ Rectangles tessellate.
4. Try lining up circles — no matter how you pack them, small curved gaps are
   left between them. ❌ Circles do NOT tessellate on their own.
5. So the answer is: square, triangle, and rectangle can tessellate; circle
   cannot.

**Try it yourself:** Can you think of one more everyday tessellating pattern
around your house (hint: look at floor tiles, a chessboard, or a honeycomb)?

## 5. Finding the "Core Unit" to Extend Any Shape Pattern

**Why it works:** Every shape pattern is built from a repeating (or steadily
changing) "core unit" — like a group of stars, arrows, or blocks. Once you
figure out exactly how the core unit changes from one step to the next, you
can predict the next step without guessing.

**Step-by-step example — Cylinder towers: 1, then 3, then 6 cylinders.**
1. Find the difference between steps: 3 − 1 = 2, and 6 − 3 = 3.
2. Notice the amount added is going up by 1 each time (add 2, then add 3).
3. So the next jump should be adding 4 more.
4. 6 + 4 = **10** cylinders in the next tower.
5. Check: is the pattern growing (getting bigger) or reducing (getting
   smaller)? Since it's getting bigger, it's a **growing** pattern.

**Try it yourself:** A diamond zig-zag pattern goes 1, then 3, then 5
diamonds. What is the core-unit rule, and how many diamonds should be in the
next group?

## 6. The "Find the Gap First" Trick for Number Patterns

**Why it works:** Almost every number pattern has a hidden rule connecting
each number to the next. The fastest way to find that rule is to subtract
each number from the one after it and look at the gaps — sometimes the gaps
are all the same, and sometimes the gaps themselves follow their own pattern!

**Step-by-step example — What is the rule for 9, 13, 18, 24, 31?**
1. Subtract each pair of neighbours: 13−9=4, 18−13=5, 24−18=6, 31−24=7.
2. Notice the gaps themselves are a pattern: 4, 5, 6, 7 — increasing by 1
   each time.
3. So the rule is: "add 4, then add 5, then add 6, then add 7…" (the amount
   added grows by 1 every step).
4. Using this rule, the next gap would be 8, so the next number after 31
   would be 31 + 8 = 39.

**Try it yourself:** Find the gaps in this pattern: 101, 91, 81, 71. Is the
gap the same every time, or does it change? What is the rule, and what comes
next?

## 7. The Sum-of-Two-Below Trick for Pyramid Puzzles

**Why it works:** In a number pyramid puzzle, each brick is built from the two
bricks directly underneath it — usually by *adding* them together. If you
know this rule, you can work forwards from the bottom OR backwards from the
top to fill in any missing brick.

**Step-by-step example — Bottom row: 13, ?, 17, ?; then 28, ?, 36; then 60,
68; top: 128.**
1. Since 60 + 68 = 128 (check: it matches the top brick), the rule really is
   "add the two bricks below."
2. Work on row 3: 28 + ? = 60, so the missing brick is 60 − 28 = 32.
3. Check the other side: ? + 36 = 68, so the missing brick is 68 − 36 = 32 — it
   matches!
4. Now go down to the bottom row: 13 + ? = 28, so the missing number is
   28 − 13 = **15**.
5. Check the other side of that row: 17 + ? = 36, so the missing number is
   36 − 17 = **19**.
6. Missing bottom numbers: 15 and 19.

**Try it yourself:** If a pyramid's bottom row is 5, 8, 11, and each brick
above is the sum of the two below, what should the two bricks in the row
above be?

## 8. Cracking Secret Letter-and-Number Codes

**Why it works:** Some "decoding" patterns hide a message by repeating one
extra letter or digit in between every real letter. Once you spot which
character keeps repeating (it's always the *same* one, over and over), you
can simply delete it to reveal the hidden message.

**Step-by-step example — Decode: "4L4E4T4 4U4S4 4G4O4"**
1. Look for a character that shows up again and again in the same pattern —
   here, it's the digit **4**, appearing before and after every letter.
2. Since "4" isn't a real letter, it must be the "filler" being repeated.
3. Cross out every "4": L E T   U S   G O.
4. Read what's left: **LET US GO**.
5. This trick works the same way whether the filler is a digit (like 4) or a
   letter (like A) — always find the character that repeats the most and
   remove it first.

**Try it yourself:** Using the same trick, decode this message:
"ARAEASAPAEACAT" (hint: the filler letter here is "A"). What word is hiding
inside it?
